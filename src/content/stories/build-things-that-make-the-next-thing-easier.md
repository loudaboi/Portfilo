Two projects can end the same way: they stop.

Neither becomes a company. Neither becomes a career. Neither makes much money.

From a distance, both can look like dead ends.

But the people who built them may not be back in the same place.

One finishes with little they can use again. The other leaves something real behind: a finished piece of work, a clearer understanding of a problem, a customer who trusts them, or an ability they did not have before.

The project is over. The next attempt starts somewhere else.

We usually judge work by the visible result. Did it work? Did people use it? Did it make money? Did it become something worth continuing?

That is the right place to start. Outcomes matter.

But especially when you are early, there is another question worth adding: **what did the work make easier to do next?**

A small product makes the idea easy to see. You build a tool. A few people use it. It never becomes a business.

That result should not be disguised as success.

But suppose the work forced you to turn a rough idea into something usable, show it to people with the problem, fix what broke, and finish something strangers could actually try. The next time you build, some of those steps are no longer completely new.

The same can happen with paid work. A small service might remain small. But doing the work for an actual customer can change what you understand about the problem, what people care enough to pay for, and what you are capable of delivering.

A newsletter, research project, event, or community effort can work the same way. The audience may stay small. The project may end. But the work can still leave behind something another person can see, someone you can call, or a skill you can use again.

The useful distinction is between a lesson and **carryover**.

A lesson can be invented after almost anything. Carryover has to exist outside the story you tell yourself about the experience.

Something is different now.

You can do something you could not do before. Someone has evidence of what you can do. You understand a problem well enough to make a better decision. You have a relationship, customer, body of work, or source of income that continues after the project itself.

The exact thing matters less than whether it is usable.

This matters when you have little previous work to draw on.

If you already have years of experience, a strong network, savings, an audience, or a track record, new projects begin with things accumulated from older ones.

When you do not have much of that yet, each serious piece of work has more potential to change your starting position.

It does not erase the constraints you began with. A good project does not make money appear, guarantee access, or turn a weak network into a strong one overnight.

But when you have a choice between things to work on, it is worth noticing which one leaves you better equipped for the next choice.

That changes how small work can be judged.

A project does not have to become your final company, career, or identity to matter. It can be worth doing because it gives the next thing a better chance of starting from somewhere stronger.

This argument has an obvious failure mode.

It can become a flattering excuse for wasting time.

A project fails, so you say you learned. Another one never ships, but you call it exploration. Months disappear into something that changes nothing, and afterward you search for a way to make the time sound useful.

That is not the idea.

If nothing specific became easier, more possible, or more credible because of the work, there may be nothing to carry forward. Repeating the same mistake is not progress. Collecting unfinished projects is not a strategy.

The test should be concrete.

Could you now do something you could not do before? Is there finished work someone else can inspect? Do you understand the problem well enough to make a different decision? Did the work create something that still exists after the project ends?

If the answer is no, then `it was valuable anyway` may just be a nicer description of sunk time.

The best outcome is still for the thing itself to work.

Build the product people want. Do the job well. Make the business viable. Keep going when the thing deserves to keep going.

The point is not to lower the standard for success. It is to stop asking every early project to become the final answer.

Some work is valuable because of what it becomes. Some is also valuable because of what it leaves you able to do afterward. The strongest work can do both.

So when you are deciding whether something deserves months of your life, add one question to the obvious ones:

**If this does not become the thing, what will it make easier to do next?**

A good answer does not guarantee success.

It does mean that if the project ends, the next one does not have to begin from the same place.
