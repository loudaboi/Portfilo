# Starting a Business Headline Research

_Last updated: 2026-09-07_

_Status: RESEARCH COMPLETE / HEADLINE APPROVED_

## Purpose

Research the headline as a separate editorial object after Draft 1 rather than allowing the working label `Starting a business` or an AI-generated phrase to become the title by default.

The Draft 1 file explicitly deferred headline and dek work. `Starting a business` is a working label, not a researched headline.

## Headline job from the approved style brief

The headline must identify the explanatory question or supported tension around what makes starting a business difficult.

It must not imply:

- that most founders give up;
- a universal business-failure rate;
- that persistence causes success;
- that quitting causes success;
- that the article contains a formula for entrepreneurship;
- that one kind of startup experience represents all businesses;
- that Philippine founders are uniquely disadvantaged.

The body explains a connected mechanism: early business-building requires action before outcomes are known, broader work than product creation, learning under resource constraints, and judgment about whether continued effort remains justified.

## Professional headline guidance used

### Poynter - `10 questions for your headline-writing checklist`

URL: https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2017/10-questions-for-your-headline-writing-checklist/

Relevant guidance:

- every word must be accurate;
- do not oversell or undersell;
- make the story's real point rather than a side issue or entry point;
- make the headline easily understandable;
- match the story's tone;
- use efficient, interesting language.

### Poynter - `9 tips for writing stronger headlines`

URL: https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2017/9-tips-for-writing-stronger-headlines/

Relevant guidance:

- be specific rather than vague;
- curiosity must not become concealment or overpromise;
- start from the simple subject/verb or who/what structure;
- use the relevant `why` or `how` question when it is genuinely what the story answers.

### Poynter - `10 questions to help you write better headlines`

URL: https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2011/10-questions-to-help-you-write-better-headlines/

Relevant guidance:

- the headline must accurately convey the material;
- question headlines are legitimate when the story actually answers or usefully frames that question;
- the title should work with limited surrounding context.

These are professional editing heuristics, not evidence that one syntax is universally superior.

## Human-edited headline comparisons

The purpose of these examples is to study framing decisions, not copy wording or cadence.

### Harvard Business Review - `Why Start-ups Fail`

URL: https://hbr.org/2021/05/why-start-ups-fail

Why it is useful:

This is a research-backed entrepreneurship article with a direct causal question as the headline.

Headline behavior:

- plain language;
- names the exact subject;
- no motivational hook;
- no promise of a secret or numbered formula;
- the sophistication is carried by the body and deck, not by making the title sound clever.

Transferable lesson:

A serious entrepreneurship article does not need a novelty phrase when the reader's real question is already strong.

Limitation:

The Portfilo story does not primarily answer why startups fail, so the causal `fail` framing is not transferable.

### Reuters - `US Treasury yields are rising - Why does it matter?`

URL: https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/us-treasury-yields-are-rising-why-does-it-matter-2026-09-01/

Why it is useful:

A current human-edited explainer that combines a concrete subject with the exact reader question.

Headline behavior:

- first establishes what the subject is;
- then asks the consequential question;
- gives away the information problem rather than hiding it behind intrigue.

Transferable lesson:

Explainer headlines can be literal and question-led when the question genuinely organizes the article.

Limitation:

Our story is evergreen rather than event-led, so it does not need a development clause before the question.

### Harvard Business Review - `When to Stick with Something - and When to Quit`

URL: https://hbr.org/2018/09/when-to-stick-with-something-and-when-to-quit

Why it is useful:

It frames persistence as a genuine two-sided decision rather than treating quitting as failure.

Headline behavior:

- names both sides of the tension;
- makes the decision problem explicit;
- avoids a moralized `never give up` frame.

Transferable lesson:

A tension headline is useful when both sides are central to the body.

Limitation:

Persistence versus quitting is only one section of the Portfilo story. Making it the whole headline would undersell the broader explanation of early business work and uncertainty.

### Harvard Business Review - `12 Guidelines for Deciding When to Persist, When to Quit`

URL: https://hbr.org/2012/10/12-guidelines-for-deciding-whe

Why it is useful:

Another example showing that edited business publications can state the persistence/exit decision plainly.

Limitation:

It is a guide/list format. Portfilo's article is not a decision checklist and must not promise one.

## What the headline should actually foreground

Draft 1 contains three possible headline centers:

1. `starting a business is difficult`;
2. `the founder acts before knowing which actions will work`;
3. `persistence is not automatically progress`.

The body gives the most space and explanatory coverage to the first two. The third is the strongest contradiction, but it is not the entire article.

Therefore the headline should foreground **the difficulty / uncertainty question**, while the dek can carry the persistence-versus-exit tension.

This division also prevents the headline from overpromising a `when to quit` guide.

## Candidate evaluation

### Candidate A - `What makes starting a business so hard?`

Form:

Direct explanatory question.

Strengths:

- names the exact subject and reader problem;
- understandable with no surrounding context;
- accurately matches the full body rather than one section;
- gives the article room to explain work, uncertainty, constraints, stress and persistence;
- avoids a predetermined motivational conclusion;
- lets the dek add the more distinctive persistence-versus-progress tension;
- matches the professional pattern of plain question-led explanatory headlines without imitating any exemplar's wording.

Weaknesses:

- broad and intentionally simple;
- `so hard` is conversational rather than technical;
- not highly distinctive on its own, so the dek and visual presentation must do some differentiation work.

Editorial assessment:

**APPROVED PRIMARY.**

Reason:

It is the most accurate headline for the actual evidence shape. Its simplicity is a feature rather than a failure if the body and dek carry the specificity.

### Candidate B - `Why is starting a business so hard?`

Form:

Direct causal question.

Strengths:

- equally clear;
- `why` makes the causal/explanatory job explicit;
- natural language.

Weaknesses:

- can imply that the article identifies a single cause;
- the body actually explains several interacting mechanisms rather than one `why`.

Editorial assessment:

**STRONG ALTERNATIVE / NOT SELECTED.**

Candidate A's `what makes` better accommodates multiple mechanisms.

### Candidate C - `Why starting a business takes more than persistence`

Form:

Declarative explanatory headline.

Strengths:

- carries the article's main contradiction;
- more distinctive than the direct question;
- does not say quitting is good.

Weaknesses:

- over-centers persistence, which arrives later in the body;
- makes the article sound closer to advice or Perspective;
- undersells customer acquisition, experimentation, constraints and operating conditions.

Editorial assessment:

**SECONDARY ONLY / NOT SELECTED.**

### Candidate D - `What the grind leaves out about starting a business`

Form:

Contrarian/cultural-frame headline.

Strengths:

- distinctive;
- connects to the reader's familiar `grind` language.

Weaknesses:

- makes `grind culture` the subject when the story is actually about entrepreneurship;
- implies an adversary or misconception is more central than the evidence warrants;
- has a recognizable online-commentary / AI-headline shape;
- risks aging poorly and sounding more promotional than explanatory.

Editorial assessment:

**REJECT.**

### Candidate E - `The hard part of starting a business is knowing what to keep doing`

Form:

Insight-led declarative claim.

Strengths:

- memorable;
- captures the persistence and judgment tension.

Weaknesses:

- `the hard part` is too absolute;
- the research does not establish one single hardest part across businesses;
- turns a bounded Portfilo synthesis into a universal-sounding empirical claim;
- sounds polished in exactly the way an AI-generated insight headline often does.

Editorial assessment:

**REJECT.**

### Candidate F - `Starting a business is hard. Knowing what to keep doing is harder.`

Form:

Two-beat contrast headline.

Strengths:

- punchy;
- readable;
- captures the central tension.

Weaknesses:

- `harder` is an unsupported comparison;
- uses artificial symmetry;
- reads more like social copy than an evidence-led explainer headline;
- oversells a rhetorical insight that the research does not measure.

Editorial assessment:

**REJECT.**

## Approved headline

> **What makes starting a business so hard?**

Approved by founder/editor on 2026-09-07.

Why it was selected:

- it asks exactly the question the body answers;
- it does not strengthen the evidence beyond what the packet supports;
- it remains broad enough for different business types while the body supplies scope and caveats;
- it avoids turning the story into hustle commentary or a quitting guide;
- it works out of context;
- it is plain enough that the headline is not competing with the reporting.

This decision intentionally prioritizes **accuracy, clarity and editorial fit over cleverness**.

## Headline/dek division

Headline job:

Ask the broad explanatory question.

Dek job:

Carry the distinctive answer and tension: early business-building requires acting before the outcome is known, paying for the process of learning, and deciding when persistence is still producing evidence rather than merely consuming more resources.

The exact dek remains a separate research/editing gate.

## Boundary

This approval applies only to this story.

Do not treat this headline as a recurring Portfilo formula for future Explained articles.
