# Starting a Business Evidence Ledger

_Last updated: 2026-09-07_

## C01

Candidate claim:

Early startups often require founders to recruit initial users manually rather than waiting for a product launch to create demand automatically.

Evidence:

Paul Graham's `Do Things That Don't Scale` describes manual user recruitment as one of the most common early unscalable tasks in the YC startup context.

Source:

S001 - Paul Graham, `Do Things That Don't Scale`.

Tier:

Primary practitioner source.

Confidence:

MEDIUM

Caveat:

Strong practitioner observation for technology startups; not a population-level result and not automatically applicable to every small business.

Status:

QUALIFY

## C02

Candidate claim:

Starting a startup involves more kinds of work than building the product itself.

Evidence:

Sam Altman's Startup Playbook separates idea, team, product, execution, growth, focus, CEO work, hiring, competition, making money, and fundraising. Steve Blank's customer-development writing similarly treats customer, pricing, channel, and business-model assumptions as work that must be tested outside product development.

Sources:

S003, S004, S005.

Tier:

Primary practitioner sources.

Confidence:

HIGH for accurately describing these practitioners' frameworks; MEDIUM as a generalization beyond startup contexts.

Caveat:

These frameworks were developed for startups, particularly innovation-driven companies.

Status:

QUALIFY

## C03

Candidate claim:

New ventures operate under substantial uncertainty, so early business-building includes learning whether the idea and strategy are viable rather than merely executing a known plan.

Evidence:

Nanda and Rhodes-Kropf describe the potential of early innovative ventures as fundamentally uncertain and entrepreneurship as a process of experimentation over time. Steve Blank's customer-development framework independently makes a practitioner version of the same distinction: initial business-model elements are hypotheses to test rather than facts to execute blindly.

Sources:

S005, S010.

Tier:

Tier A research plus primary practitioner methodology.

Confidence:

HIGH within innovative/startup venture scope.

Caveat:

The degree of uncertainty varies by business type. A proven local service franchise and a novel technology startup do not face identical uncertainty.

Status:

VERIFIED / SCOPE REQUIRED

## C04

Candidate claim:

Business closure is common over a multi-year horizon.

Evidence:

BLS Business Employment Dynamics data show five-year survival rates around half for several U.S. startup-establishment cohorts. BLS reported that 34.7% of U.S. private-sector establishments born in March 2013 were still operating in March 2023.

Source:

S007 - U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Tier:

A

Confidence:

HIGH for the defined U.S. establishment populations.

Caveat:

Establishment closure is not the same thing as a founder `giving up`; the data do not by themselves reveal motive, sale, pivot, merger, retirement, or whether a founder later starts another business.

Status:

VERIFIED

## C05

Candidate claim:

`Most founders give up` is not currently supportable as a general factual statement.

Evidence:

Available survival datasets measure firm or establishment continuation, not a founder's psychological act of `giving up`. GEM also distinguishes different forms and reasons for business exit, and research on entrepreneurial quitting treats shutdown as potentially rational learning rather than simple weakness.

Sources:

S007, S008, S009.

Tier:

A / A-B

Confidence:

HIGH

Caveat:

A narrower dataset could potentially support a statement about discontinuation in a defined population, but that would still require precise language rather than `give up`.

Status:

REJECT AS CURRENT GENERAL CLAIM

## C06

Candidate claim:

Persistence is not always the correct entrepreneurial response; learning can make earlier shutdown rational for less viable ventures.

Evidence:

A 2026 randomized field experiment involving 553 science- and technology-based startups found that entrepreneurship training increased shutdown rates and accelerated shutdown, while surviving treated firms raised funding faster and more, and had higher employment and revenue. The authors interpret the pattern as consistent with `rationally quit` / fail-fast learning.

Source:

S009 - NBER `Learning to Quit?`.

Tier:

A research source.

Confidence:

HIGH for the experiment; MEDIUM for broader generalization.

Caveat:

The sample was U.S. innovation-driven startups. The study does not prove that quitting is generally superior to persistence or that shutdown causes better outcomes.

Status:

VERIFIED / QUALIFY

## C07

Candidate claim:

Entrepreneurial work can make psychological detachment and recovery difficult.

Evidence:

A Journal of Business Venturing diary study found daily entrepreneurial stressors inhibited psychological detachment and reduced next-morning well-being. A 2026 co-twin analysis found self-employment positively associated with stress, with long work hours partially explaining the relationship.

Sources:

S011, S012.

Tier:

A peer-reviewed research.

Confidence:

MEDIUM-HIGH

Caveat:

Samples and measures differ; self-employment is heterogeneous. The evidence does not justify saying that all founders are burned out or unhappy.

Status:

VERIFIED / QUALIFY

## C08

Candidate claim:

Hardship and dissatisfaction are not the same thing.

Evidence:

Research on self-employment has found high reports of pressure, strain, worry, and exhaustion alongside high perceived control and life satisfaction. The entrepreneur-stressor diary literature also distinguishes challenge stressors that can have positive associations from hindrance effects.

Sources:

S011, S013.

Tier:

A / A-B research.

Confidence:

MEDIUM

Caveat:

Older self-employment data and heterogeneous populations. Use as counterevidence against one-dimensional `entrepreneurship is misery` framing, not as a universal positive claim.

Status:

QUALIFY

## C09

Candidate claim:

The Philippine business context is dominated numerically by smaller establishments, while local firms and startups face structural constraints that can make growth harder.

Evidence:

PSA formal-sector data show micro and small establishments make up the large majority of establishment counts. ADB identifies funding, talent/mentorship, digital infrastructure, and digital literacy as constraints in the Philippine technology-startup ecosystem. World Bank work identifies regulatory complexity, competition, finance, and technology-adoption constraints affecting Philippine firm dynamism and scaling.

Sources:

S014, S015, S016.

Tier:

A / A-B institutional evidence.

Confidence:

HIGH for the cited structural findings.

Caveat:

These sources do not directly measure individual founder hardship or business survival. They should supply Philippine context only where it materially helps answer the story question.

Status:

VERIFIED / CONTEXT ONLY
