What actually makes starting a business difficult, beyond simply working long hours?

A new business has to make important decisions before it has much evidence. The owner is choosing what to sell, what to charge, who the customer is, how to reach them, and how much money to commit while still finding out whether those choices make sense. Learning happens while the business is already spending time and cash.

Weak sales are a simple example. They do not tell the owner what is wrong. Demand may be weak. The price may be wrong. The offer may be unclear, or the business may not be reaching the right customers yet. Another week of sales or a few customer conversations can provide more information, but no single signal settles the question.

Early business work often includes selling directly, changing the offer, trying another price, or doing something manually before spending money on a larger system. The purpose is not to stay busy. It is to get better information about whether customers will buy and whether the business can serve them at a workable cost.

That learning is expensive because the business is not a classroom exercise. A wrong assumption can leave inventory unsold, advertising money wasted, or cash tied up in equipment, rent, or people before demand is clear. Even useful information can arrive after the business has already paid for it.

In the Philippines, the World Bank's [2023 Enterprise Survey](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/content/dam/enterprisesurveys/documents/country/Philippines-2023.pdf) gives a useful example of how operating conditions add to that cost. Formal firms covered by the survey reported obstacles including access to finance, electricity, business permits, and transportation. The survey covers registered private firms with at least five workers in eligible sectors, so it does not represent every microbusiness or informal business. It also does not explain why any particular company succeeds or fails. What it does show is that a business can be trying to understand its customers and economics while also dealing with constraints outside the product itself.

More effort can help when the business still has something important to learn or improve. But repeated effort can also keep consuming money without changing the underlying problem.

A 2026 randomized study called [`Learning to Quit?`](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34755) is useful here. It followed 553 science- and technology-based startups. Founders who received entrepreneurship training became more likely to shut down and did so sooner. Among the ventures that survived, the trained group later showed stronger outcomes in funding, employment, and revenue. The authors interpret the pattern as consistent with some founders learning to stop weaker ventures earlier.

The study does not show that closing a business causes success, and its sample does not represent every kind of business. It does show why persistence by itself is not evidence that a venture is improving.

There is no universal number of months, sales calls, or failed attempts that separates useful persistence from wasted effort. Different businesses have different margins, cash requirements, sales cycles, and alternatives. The owner still has to make the next decision before all of that uncertainty is gone.
