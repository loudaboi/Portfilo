Starting a business is hard. That sentence is true enough to be useless.

It does not tell you what the difficulty is made of. Long hours are part of some businesses, but hours alone do not explain why a new company can feel uncertain even when the founder is working constantly. The harder problem is that much of the work happens before anyone knows which parts will matter.

A new business has to make decisions about customers, price, distribution, cash, hiring and operations while still learning whether the underlying idea works. The founder can be busy and still be wrong. A slow week can mean weak demand, bad pricing, poor distribution, a bad offer, seasonal noise or something else entirely. Some problems call for more effort. Others call for a different decision.

That is a more useful way to understand the early `grind`: **starting a business means acting under uncertainty while paying the cost of learning what is true.**

The cost can be manual selling, wrong assumptions, limited money, permits, unreliable infrastructure, stress, wasted time, or a product that never finds enough demand. The difficulty is not just enduring those problems. It is deciding what they are telling you.

## The work is larger than the product

Startup writing often begins with the product because the product is visible. The early work around it is less visible.

Paul Graham's [`Do Things That Don't Scale`](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html), written from the Y Combinator startup context, argues that founders often have to recruit their first users manually rather than wait for a launch to create demand. Steve Blank's [Customer Development](https://steveblank.com/2009/08/31/the-customer-development-manifesto-reasons-for-the-revolution-part-1/) framework treats customers, pricing, channels and the business model as hypotheses that have to be tested outside the office. Sam Altman's [Startup Playbook](https://playbook.samaltman.com/) spreads the work across product, execution, growth, hiring, money, fundraising and focus.

These are practitioner frameworks, not laws that every business must follow. A restaurant, agency, family retailer and software startup do not share the same economics. But the frameworks point to a practical distinction that survives beyond venture-backed technology companies: creating the thing is only one part of discovering whether a business exists around it.

A product can work technically and still fail to attract enough customers. People can like an idea and refuse to pay the proposed price. A founder can find buyers but discover that serving them costs too much. A sales channel can work until the cost of acquiring a customer rises. The early job is partly production and partly measurement.

That is why some of the least glamorous work can carry the most information. A direct sales conversation can reveal that the buyer does not understand the offer. A rejected proposal can expose a price problem. A manual process can show what should eventually be automated. None of those observations guarantees a better business. They are useful because they turn assumptions into evidence.

## You have to move before the answer is known

Economists have described entrepreneurship as a form of experimentation for a similar reason.

An NBER paper on [`Entrepreneurship as Experimentation`](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20358) argues that the potential of an innovative venture is often difficult to know before investment and market exposure. A related paper on [`Financing Entrepreneurial Experimentation`](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21278) treats investment as sequential because entrepreneurs and investors learn over time whether a venture deserves more resources.

The mechanism is not limited to high-growth software companies. Research on [microenterprise development](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18325) has examined how financial and managerial constraints can limit an owner's ability to experiment and learn about profitability. Research on [self-employment dynamics](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23168) similarly models people learning over time about uncertain entrepreneurial earnings.

This does not mean every founder should run a scientific experiment on every decision. Entrepreneurship researchers have also argued that the `entrepreneur as scientist` metaphor has limits when problems cannot be cleanly decomposed or measured. Some uncertainty cannot be resolved by an A/B test or a neat spreadsheet.

The useful point is narrower. A new business is not always executing a plan whose result is already known. Part of the work is finding out whether the plan deserves to survive contact with customers, costs and competition.

That changes the meaning of effort. Ten more hours spent polishing something customers do not want may produce less useful information than one uncomfortable sales conversation. Continuing the same action is not automatically persistence in a productive sense. Sometimes it is simply more exposure to the same mistake.

## Constraints make learning expensive

Uncertainty would be easier to tolerate if every experiment were cheap. It usually is not.

A business pays for learning with time and money. Inventory has to be purchased. Employees have to be paid. A founder may have limited savings or limited time before the business has to support itself. A bad location, delayed permit, unreliable electricity or financing problem can consume resources without resolving the central question of demand.

The Philippine operating environment makes some of those constraints concrete. The World Bank's [2023 Enterprise Survey for the Philippines](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/content/dam/enterprisesurveys/documents/country/Philippines-2023.pdf) lists electricity, competition from the informal sector, access to finance, business licenses and permits, tax rates and transportation among the business-environment obstacles reported by formal firms. The survey also shows substantial reliance on internal finance for investment.

Those results should not be stretched too far. The Enterprise Survey covers formal private firms with at least five employees, so it does not represent every sari-sari store, solo operator or informal microbusiness. It also measures reported business constraints, not why any particular founder succeeds or fails.

Broader World Bank work on [Philippine growth and jobs](https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/philippines/publication/philippines-growth-and-jobs-report-2025) identifies regulatory complexity, competition, financing, skills scarcity, technology adoption and climate shocks as constraints on firm dynamism and scaling. A 2026 World Bank [COMPETE for SMEs](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099012126091536048/pdf/P507827-6119c7d0-f4ac-40e0-9152-251cf951050f.pdf) document points to business and technical skills, certifications, market intelligence, business connections and long-term finance among SME competitiveness constraints.

This is where imported startup advice can become too clean. `Talk to users` may still be good advice, but the owner also has to deal with the conditions under which the business operates. A sound offer can still face financing constraints. A good product can still depend on transport, power, regulation or access to skilled people. Business uncertainty sits inside an operating environment.

There is a psychological cost as well. A peer-reviewed [diary study of 55 entrepreneurs](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0883902618308358) found that daily work stressors interfered with psychological detachment from work and were associated with lower well-being the following morning. A 2026 [co-twin study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0883902625000849) found a positive association between self-employment and stress, with long working hours explaining part of the relationship.

That does not make entrepreneurship uniformly miserable. Older self-employment research has found high pressure and exhaustion alongside high perceived control and life satisfaction. Difficulty and dissatisfaction are not the same thing.

The relevant point is that learning is costly when the person making the decisions is also carrying the financial and operational consequences.

## Persistence is not the same thing as progress

This is where the usual `never quit` story breaks down.

Business closure is common, but closure data do not tell us that founders simply gave up. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that [34.7% of private-sector establishments born in March 2013 were still operating in March 2023](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm). Five-year survival rates for several establishment cohorts are around half.

Those are establishment-survival statistics. They do not tell us whether an owner sold, changed direction, retired, merged operations, started another business or decided that the opportunity was no longer worth pursuing. Survival is also not the same thing as success. A business can remain open while producing poor returns, and a business can close after creating value.

Research on entrepreneurial quitting makes the distinction sharper.

A 2026 randomized field experiment, [`Learning to Quit?`](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34755), studied 553 science- and technology-based startups in 12 U.S. co-working spaces. Entrepreneurship training increased the probability and speed of shutdown. Among the ventures that survived, treated startups subsequently raised funding faster and raised more of it, and reported higher employment and revenue. The authors interpret the pattern as consistent with founders learning to shut down weaker ventures sooner.

The study does not prove that quitting causes success, and its sample is not representative of all businesses. It does show why persistence cannot be treated as an unconditional virtue.

Other entrepreneurship research finds that persistence decisions themselves can be distorted. Experiments with entrepreneurs in Australia and the United Kingdom found that [past underperformance and prior personal investment can influence decisions to continue](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0883902622000222). A review of the persistence literature describes the concept as more complicated than a simple positive trait.

The founder's problem is therefore awkward: a new business often needs enough time and repeated effort to produce meaningful evidence, but the same history of effort can make it harder to stop.

There is no universal number of months, sales calls or failed experiments that resolves that tension. Different businesses have different capital requirements, sales cycles, margins, regulations and alternatives. The evidence supports a principle, not a formula: continued effort should earn its place by producing information, customers, improving economics or some other defensible reason to believe the business is becoming more viable.

## What the `grind` actually contains

The popular image of the grind focuses on endurance because endurance is easy to see. The less visible part is judgment.

Starting a business can require doing manual work before systems exist, selling before the offer feels finished, paying for mistakes, learning what customers actually value, coping with constraints outside the product and continuing through periods where the signal is weak.

It can also require abandoning an assumption that took months to build, changing a product that the founder likes, admitting that a market is smaller than expected, or closing a venture that no longer justifies more time and money.

That is why `work harder` is an incomplete explanation of the difficulty. The founder is not only trying to produce more output. The founder is trying to learn which output deserves to continue.

Persistence matters when the next round of effort can reveal something useful or improve the business. It becomes harder to defend when the evidence keeps getting worse and the only argument left is the amount already invested.

Starting a business is difficult partly because those two situations can feel similar while you are inside them.
