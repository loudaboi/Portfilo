What actually makes starting a business difficult, beyond the workload?

A new business has to make decisions before it fully understands its own results. Low sales are an easy example. They tell the owner that not enough sales happened. They do not say whether the price is wrong, the offer is weak, the wrong people are seeing it, too few people know about it, or demand is simply lower than expected.

Those possibilities call for different responses. Cutting the price can help if customers want the product but think it costs too much. If the real problem is that too few people know the business exists, the same price cut only reduces the money earned on each sale. Advertising can help an awareness problem, but it can also spend money faster if the offer itself is weak. Producing more inventory makes sense when demand is being missed; it creates a larger problem when the stock already on hand is not moving.

The business usually becomes easier to read only after more real activity produces more clues. Some people may ask about the product and disappear after hearing the price. More visitors may see the offer without improving sales. One product may keep selling while another sits. Existing customers may return even while new-customer sales remain weak. None of those observations explains the business by itself, but together they can narrow the possible causes.

The owner is paying for that learning while it happens. Inventory can sit unsold. Advertising can fail. Rent, payroll, software, equipment, and the owner's own time continue to cost money while the picture is still incomplete. A wrong diagnosis can therefore be more expensive than the original bad result: it can send more money toward something that was never the main problem.

Weak sales are the result. The cause is still something the business has to learn. That distinction matters because effort is not interchangeable. More marketing, lower prices, more inventory, longer hours, or a different offer can each be sensible under the right conditions and wasteful under the wrong ones.

During the early stage, running the business and understanding the business happen at the same time. The owner still has to act before the diagnosis is complete, which is why simply working harder cannot solve every early business problem. More effort helps when it is aimed at the right cause or when the effort itself produces better information about what the cause might be.
