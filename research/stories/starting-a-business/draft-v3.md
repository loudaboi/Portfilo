Starting a business means making decisions before you have enough information to know whether those decisions are right.

You have to decide what to sell, who to sell it to, what to charge, how to reach customers, how much to spend and what to change when something is not working. Early on, the business has very little history to help answer those questions.

That is a large part of what makes the beginning difficult.

Working long hours can be part of it, but effort does not tell you whether the problem is your product, your price, your sales process, your location, your costs or simply that not enough people know you exist yet. You still have to make the next decision while you are figuring that out.

## You start with guesses

Before a business has real customers and real sales, many of its important decisions are still assumptions.

You might believe people want the product. You might think the price is reasonable. You might expect Facebook, a physical location, referrals or direct sales to bring customers in. You might estimate how much each sale will cost you to deliver.

Some of those assumptions will be right. Others will not.

Suppose you open and sales are weak. That does not immediately tell you why. The product could be wrong for the market. The price could be too high. The offer might be confusing. The right customers might not have seen it yet. One slow week may not tell you much at all.

So part of running a new business is collecting better information while the business is already operating.

That can mean talking to customers, trying a different price, changing the offer, testing another way to reach people or doing something manually before paying to build a system around it.

The goal is not to keep changing things for the sake of it. It is to learn enough to make the next decision with better information than you had before.

## Every wrong guess costs something

Learning in a business is not free.

A bad decision can mean unsold inventory, wasted advertising money, time spent building something customers do not want or cash tied up in the wrong place. If employees, rent, equipment or debt are involved, the cost of being wrong becomes larger.

The environment around the business matters too.

In the Philippines, the World Bank's [2023 Enterprise Survey](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/content/dam/enterprisesurveys/documents/country/Philippines-2023.pdf) found that formal firms reported obstacles including access to finance, electricity, business permits and transportation. The survey covers registered private firms with at least five workers in eligible sectors, so it does not describe every small or informal business. But it shows that a business can be trying to figure out its customers while also dealing with problems that have nothing to do with the product itself.

This is one reason the early stage can feel unforgiving. A founder is learning, but the rent still has to be paid. Cash still runs down. Customers can still leave. A mistake can teach you something and still hurt the business.

That also puts pressure on the person making the decisions. The same person trying to understand why sales are weak may also be worrying about payroll, suppliers or how long the remaining cash will last.

## More effort is not always progress

The usual advice around entrepreneurship puts a lot of weight on persistence. Sometimes that is necessary. A new business often needs time to find customers, improve the offer and learn how to operate.

But staying open longer does not automatically mean the business is getting healthier.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that [34.7% of private-sector establishments created in March 2013 were still operating in March 2023](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm). That does not mean the others simply "gave up." A business can close for many reasons, and survival by itself does not tell you whether the owner was earning a good return or whether continuing was the right decision.

A more useful example comes from a 2026 study called [`Learning to Quit?`](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34755). Researchers studied 553 science- and technology-based startups. Founders who received entrepreneurship training became more likely to shut down and did so sooner. Among the businesses that remained, the trained group also showed stronger outcomes on measures such as funding, employment and revenue.

The study does not show that closing a business causes success. Its authors argue that the pattern is consistent with some founders learning earlier that their venture was not worth continuing.

That matters because it changes what "persistence" means.

If something is not working, more effort can help when the business still has something important to learn or improve. But more effort can also mean spending more money on the same weak assumption.

There is no universal rule that tells a founder exactly when those two situations have become different. A restaurant, an agency, a retail shop and a software company have different margins, costs and sales cycles.

The difficulty is that the decision still has to be made.

A new business owner keeps choosing what to change, what to spend, what to keep doing and what to stop before the business has given a clear answer. The work is hard partly because learning what works happens at the same time as paying for the consequences of being wrong.
