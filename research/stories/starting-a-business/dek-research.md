# Starting a Business Dek Research

_Last updated: 2026-09-07_

_Status: DEK APPROVED / DESCRIPTION NOT YET APPROVED_

## Approved headline

> **What actually makes starting a business hard?**

## Approved body direction

Draft 8 is approved.

The article explains that a new business has to make consequential decisions while still learning what its observable results actually mean. Weak sales are the main explanatory case. The reader should understand that a result does not automatically identify its cause, that different causes require different responses, and that the next action can be useful partly for what it helps the business learn before more resources are committed.

## Initial dek job

Per `docs/COPY_STYLE.md`, the dek must add information rather than translate the headline into another sentence.

The initial pass tried to keep the headline broad while adding the article's result-versus-cause mechanism and resource stakes.

## Rejected candidates

All initial candidates were rejected by the founder/editor as too weak.

Rejected Candidate A:

> New owners have to decide where to spend time and money while still learning what their results actually mean.

Rejected Candidate B:

> A new business has to keep operating while the owner is still figuring out what caused the results they can see.

Rejected Candidate C:

> A bad result can have several causes, and acting on the wrong one can waste limited time and money.

Rejected Candidate D:

> Weak sales can point to price, demand, awareness, distribution or the offer itself. The hard part is deciding what to change before the cause is clear.

## Why the initial pass failed

The candidates were accurate, but accuracy alone was not enough.

They behaved like compressed body summaries. They explained the article's mechanism without giving the headline/dek pair a stronger reason to read.

The dek must distinguish between:

- **summary:** what the body says;
- **dek:** useful additional context that helps the headline/editorial package orient the reader.

The dek should sharpen the story's scope without becoming clickbait, a slogan, a second headline, a mini-guide, or a compressed thesis.

## Rejected tension-led correction

A later pass approved a **tension-led** direction after comparing it with using no dek.

That direction was subsequently rejected by the founder/editor because it produced forced, aphorism-shaped wording that sounded like copywriting rather than natural editorial framing.

The failure was not merely candidate quality. The structural instruction itself encouraged lines built around rhetorical oppositions such as `X before Y`, `something is wrong but...`, or other manufactured tensions.

Do not return to tension-led wording unless the founder/editor explicitly reopens it.

## Approved re-research direction

Founder/editor approved on 2026-09-07:

> **Use a plain, subject-led scope/context dek.**

The headline already supplies the broad question and curiosity:

> **What actually makes starting a business hard?**

The dek's job is therefore not to create another tension or restate the article's thesis. It should stay with the subject of **starting a business** and tell the reader, in ordinary editorial language, which part of that experience this explainer examines.

The headline and dek should be treated as one editorial package. The dek does not need to function as a quotable standalone line.

### Preferred behavior

The dek may add:

- the specific aspect of starting a business examined by the article;
- the practical scope of the explanation;
- plain context that narrows the broad headline without giving away the entire body.

### Reject wording that behaves like

- an aphorism;
- a clever observation designed to stand alone;
- a slogan;
- `X before Y` or `not X but Y` rhetoric;
- `the hard part is...` phrasing that merely answers the headline in compressed form;
- a restatement of `result != cause`;
- a list of the possible causes of weak sales;
- a recap of the article's resource stakes;
- a summary of the closing lesson;
- generic entrepreneurship advice unrelated to Draft 8.

Professional examples may justify editorial behavior, but their wording, cadence, branded voice, or distinctive constructions must not be imitated.

**No dek remains the fallback.** If a subject-led scope/context line cannot add useful orientation without repeating the headline or body, Portfilo should publish the headline without a dek rather than force one.

## Human-edited exemplar check

Research completed after the subject-led correction focused on how established business/explainer publications use the line beneath a broad business or entrepreneurship headline.

### Professional guidance

Poynter, `Hierarchy Within Headlines: Layers of Storytelling`:

https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2002/hierarchy-within-headlines-layers-of-storytelling/

Transferable lesson: additional decks should flesh out the story and add information beyond the main headline. Poynter also explicitly notes that not every headline needs multiple decks.

### Bloomberg Businessweek

`Business Plan in Hand, a Founder Starts Searching for Something to Sell`:

https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-08-11/business-plan-in-hand-a-founder-starts-searching-for-something-to-sell

Observed behavior: the subhead stays with the founder and the practical startup problem. It adds concrete context about what proved difficult instead of trying to become a separate aphorism or generic entrepreneurship thesis.

Transferable lesson: under a specific or broad business headline, the supporting line can simply locate the reader inside the actual business situation.

### WIRED

`Entrepreneur First doesn't find startups, it builds them`:

https://www.wired.com/story/entrepreneur-first-startups-london/

Observed behavior: the dek plainly explains what Entrepreneur First does and the scope of the story. The line depends on the headline and does not try to function as a standalone slogan.

Transferable lesson: a useful dek can be descriptive and subordinate to the headline rather than rhetorically self-contained.

### Harvard Business Review

`So You Want to Be an Entrepreneur?`:

https://hbr.org/2020/07/so-you-want-to-be-an-entrepreneur

`Making the Leap to Entrepreneurship`:

https://hbr.org/2020/04/making-the-leap-to-entrepreneurship

Observed behavior: HBR often uses a visible `Summary` layer rather than a conventional one-line dek. In these entrepreneurship examples, that layer begins by orienting the reader around starting the business and the specific issue the article will address.

Transferable lesson: the supporting copy does not need to manufacture a clever second proposition. Its useful job can be to narrow the subject and tell the reader what part of entrepreneurship the piece examines.

### Result for this story

The examples support the founder/editor correction rather than the earlier tension-led rule.

For this article, candidate deks should:

1. begin from the subject of starting or running a new business;
2. narrow the broad headline toward the early operating/learning problem actually covered by Draft 8;
3. use ordinary editorial language;
4. avoid trying to sound memorable by itself;
5. leave the weak-sales explanation and the article's final lesson to the body.
