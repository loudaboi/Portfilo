Starting a business is hard. That sentence is true enough to be useless.

The difficulty is not simply long hours. A new business has to make decisions about customers, price, distribution, cash and operations before there is much evidence about which decisions will work. A quiet week might point to weak demand, poor distribution, the wrong price, seasonal noise or a problem with the offer. The founder still has to act before those explanations can be separated.

Uncertainty is therefore part of the work itself. Starting a business often means spending real time and money to learn whether customers want the offer, whether the economics can work and whether the next round of effort is likely to reveal something new.

That same uncertainty makes persistence difficult to judge. Continued effort can be necessary, but continuation alone is not evidence that the business is becoming more viable.

## Building the thing is only part of startup work

Some of the best-known startup advice focuses on work that happens outside the product.

Paul Graham's [`Do Things That Don't Scale`](https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html), written from the Y Combinator startup context, argues that founders often recruit early users manually instead of waiting for a launch to create demand. Steve Blank's [Customer Development](https://steveblank.com/2009/08/31/the-customer-development-manifesto-reasons-for-the-revolution-part-1/) framework treats assumptions about customers, pricing, channels and the business model as things to test rather than facts already known.

Those are startup frameworks, not rules for every restaurant, agency, retailer, franchise or family business. They are useful here for a narrower reason: even in product-driven startups, finishing the product does not settle the commercial questions around it.

A product can work technically and still struggle to attract enough buyers. People can express interest and still reject the price. A sales conversation can provide a signal about how an offer is understood, but one conversation does not establish the cause of weak demand. Repeated customer contact can help test an assumption before a company builds more systems around it.

The early work can therefore include selling, listening, changing the offer and doing manually what might later be automated. The value of that work is not that it is difficult or unscalable. It is that it can produce information the business did not have before.

## A new business is learning while it operates

Entrepreneurship research often treats this as a learning problem under uncertainty.

An NBER paper on [`Entrepreneurship as Experimentation`](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20358) argues that the potential of an innovative venture can be difficult to know before investment and market exposure. Related work on [microenterprise development](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18325) and [self-employment dynamics](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23168) extends the learning problem beyond venture-backed technology startups: owners can invest resources while still learning about profitability or their likely returns from entrepreneurship.

The degree of uncertainty differs by business. Opening another branch of a proven franchise is not the same problem as creating a new software category. Nor can every decision be reduced to a clean experiment. Entrepreneurship research has also warned against treating the founder as a scientist who can isolate and measure every important variable.

The narrower point holds: a business plan begins with claims about demand, price, costs, distribution and operations that market experience may confirm, weaken or leave unresolved. Work and learning happen at the same time.

That is why effort can be difficult to interpret. More time spent on the same approach may help when the business still lacks enough information. It may add little when the underlying assumption has already been tested repeatedly and the result has not improved. From inside the business, those situations are not always easy to distinguish.

## Learning has a cost

A business pays for uncertainty with real resources.

Inventory has to be purchased. Employees have to be paid. A founder may have limited savings or limited time before the business has to support itself. Financing, permits, transport or unreliable infrastructure can consume resources even when the underlying product has not changed.

The Philippine operating environment makes some of those constraints concrete. The World Bank's [2023 Enterprise Survey for the Philippines](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/content/dam/enterprisesurveys/documents/country/Philippines-2023.pdf) lists electricity, competition from the informal sector, access to finance, business licenses and permits, tax rates and transportation among the business-environment obstacles reported by firms. It also shows substantial reliance on internal finance for investment.

The survey covers registered private firms with at least five workers in eligible sectors. It does not represent every microbusiness or explain why a particular company succeeds or fails. Its value here is narrower: business learning happens inside an operating environment, and that environment can make mistakes, delays and experimentation more expensive.

There is a human cost as well. A peer-reviewed [diary study of 55 entrepreneurs](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0883902618308358) found that daily work stressors interfered with psychological detachment from work and were associated with lower well-being the following morning. Other research on self-employment has found that pressure and exhaustion can coexist with high perceived control and satisfaction. Hardship is not the same thing as uniform misery.

For a business owner, the person trying to interpret the signal is often the same person carrying the financial and operational consequences of getting it wrong.

## Persistence is another decision under uncertainty

Business closure is common, but closure statistics do not tell us that founders simply gave up.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that [34.7% of private-sector establishments born in March 2013 were still operating in March 2023](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm). That is an establishment-survival figure. It does not reveal whether an owner sold, changed direction, retired, merged operations, moved into another business or concluded that the opportunity was no longer worth pursuing. Survival is not a direct measure of success either.

A 2026 randomized field experiment, [`Learning to Quit?`](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34755), studied 553 science- and technology-based startups in 12 U.S. co-working spaces. Entrepreneurship training increased the probability and speed of shutdown. Separately, among ventures that survived, treated startups showed stronger observed outcomes in funding, employment and revenue. The authors interpret the combination as consistent with founders learning to shut down weaker ventures sooner.

The study does not show that shutting down caused the stronger later performance, and its sample does not represent every kind of business. It does show why persistence cannot be treated as proof that a venture deserves more resources.

Continuation decisions can also be affected by what has already been invested. Experiments with entrepreneurs in Australia and the United Kingdom found that [past underperformance and prior personal investment influenced decisions to continue](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0883902622000222). The history of effort can become part of the decision about whether to spend more.

This creates an awkward problem. A new business may need repeated attempts before demand, pricing or operations become clear. The same repetition can also consume more money and time without changing the underlying economics.

## The uncertainty does not end with effort

The popular idea of the grind emphasizes endurance because endurance is visible. The less visible work is interpretation.

A founder may be selling before the offer feels finished, doing work manually before systems exist, paying for mistakes and trying to separate a temporary problem from a weak business model. Some results provide useful information. Others are noise. Some setbacks are operational. Others point to demand or economics that may not improve with more effort.

There is no universal number of months, sales calls or failed attempts that separates productive persistence from costly continuation. Different businesses have different margins, sales cycles, capital requirements, regulations and alternatives.

That is what makes the difficulty harder to reduce to a slogan about working more or refusing to quit. Starting a business requires action before the answer is known, and the next decision often has to be made before the uncertainty is gone.
