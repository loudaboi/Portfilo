## Why this pass exists

Draft 6 improved the story's message but still read as AI-shaped at paragraph level.

The failure was not the approved high-level form. The failure was execution: repeated one-sentence declarations, repeated setup -> example -> mini-conclusion cycles, and a tendency to state the implication after every small example.

This pass studies how human-edited explainers actually move through difficult material after the central answer is established.

## Approved reader destination

Source: `reader-destination-decision.md`

The reader should understand that a business result tells the owner what happened, not automatically what caused it, and that this makes early business decisions difficult because several possible causes may require different responses.

## Professional guidance

### Poynter — explanatory journalism and making hard facts easy to read

Sources:

- https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2020/explanatory-journalism-is-entering-a-golden-age-in-the-middle-of-the-coronavirus-pandemic/
- https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2016/10-tips-for-making-hard-facts-easy-reading/

Observed guidance:

Roy Peter Clark's explanatory-writing guidance emphasizes slowing the rate of information, introducing difficult concepts one at a time, uncluttering leads, using simple sentences where complexity rises, translating jargon, considering impact, and cutting information the reader does not need.

Important implication for this story:

Complexity does not require a repeated paragraph formula. It requires control over information rate. A paragraph may simply explain one distinction, continue an example, or add a necessary consequence without also supplying a polished takeaway sentence.

### Poynter — Ed Yong explanatory example

Source:

- https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2021/what-journalism-students-can-learn-from-this-years-pulitzer-prizes/

Observed example:

Poynter highlights Ed Yong's explanatory writing because difficult distinctions are made directly and sequentially. In the quoted example about SARS-CoV-2 and COVID-19, the prose first distinguishes virus from disease, then widens the explanation to the infected person and society.

Transferable decision:

When a distinction is central, explain it directly and then extend it. Do not repeatedly stop to announce that the distinction is important.

### Poynter — variation in sentence and paragraph movement

Sources:

- https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2006/sentence-length-and-power/
- https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2008/the-well-crafted-sentence/

Observed guidance:

Sentence and paragraph rhythm varies with the job. Shorter sentences can slow the reader at a difficult point, while longer developed paragraphs can carry connected explanation. Variation prevents monotony.

Transferable decision:

Do not make every paragraph short merely because short prose appears clearer. Draft 6's many one- and two-sentence paragraphs created a repetitive AI cadence. Paragraph length should follow the amount of connected thought needed.

## Human-edited exemplar 1 — Brian Palmer / Slate Explainer

Article:

`It's 11:48 a.m. Do You Know Where Your Missile Is?`

Archive:

https://www.slate.com/media/70/04102009.pdf

Observed movement:

The first paragraph establishes the situation and ends with the exact reader question. The next paragraph answers immediately and begins explaining the decisive distinction. It does not insert a standalone thesis sentence between the question and the explanation.

The explanation then proceeds through what information each side could actually possess and what that information can establish.

Transferable lesson:

Question and answer can be tightly connected to the first necessary explanation. The article does not need a sequence of isolated declaration paragraphs such as `Here is the problem`, `Those causes matter`, `The goal is...` before continuing.

Do not imitate Slate's wording, humor, headline style, or cadence.

## Human-edited exemplar 2 — StateImpact New Hampshire

Article:

`Are Small Businesses Really An Innovative Economic Engine?`

URL:

https://stateimpact.npr.org/new-hampshire/2011/09/19/are-small-businesses-really-an-innovative-economic-engine/

Observed movement:

The article opens with familiar examples, states the conventional assumption, then overturns it. It stays with the same underlying distinction for multiple paragraphs while research and examples deepen it. It does not conclude every example with a separate sentence explaining the lesson.

The article later moves from the national research to policy and then local New Hampshire context because those changes answer genuine follow-on questions.

Transferable lesson:

A central distinction can support several developed paragraphs. Examples should accumulate understanding before the writer states the broader implication. Do not alternate mechanically between example and mini-summary.

Boundary:

The article's playful voice, jokes, famous-name opening, and dated blog cadence are not models for Portfilo.

## Human-edited exemplar 3 — Reuters inflation explainer

Article:

`The Fed nears its 2% solution after a punishing bout of rising prices`

URL:

https://www.reuters.com/markets/us/fed-nears-its-2-solution-after-punishing-bout-rising-prices-2024-07-29/

Observed movement:

Reuters uses literal reader-question sections such as `HOW BAD WAS IT?` and `WILL PRICES DROP FROM HERE?`. Inside those sections, paragraphs often develop one answer across several sentences. The prose moves from an answer, to concrete categories or examples, to why those facts matter for the reader or policy.

It does not require each paragraph to end with a thesis-like sentence. Some paragraphs simply carry explanation, evidence, or contrast.

Transferable lesson:

Paragraphs are allowed to be functional and unfinished in isolation. The section or sequence carries the larger meaning. A paragraph does not need to sound quotable or self-contained.

Do not imitate Reuters' wire cadence, heading convention, or institutional voice.

## What Draft 6 did wrong structurally

### 1. Too many declaration paragraphs

Examples of the pattern:

- `One of the hardest parts is figuring out what a result actually means.`
- `Those causes point to different decisions.`
- `So the owner has to learn while the business is already operating.`
- `The goal is to reduce that uncertainty enough to make a better next decision.`

Each sentence is defensible in isolation, but together they create a generated outline rhythm. They announce the architecture instead of letting the article move through the business problem.

### 2. Repeated micro-cycle

Draft 6 repeatedly does:

1. declare a point;
2. give an example;
3. explain the lesson;
4. start a new point.

That is a useful planning device, but it should not remain visible as the article's paragraph architecture.

### 3. Excessive one-sentence paragraphs

Short paragraphs can create emphasis, but Draft 6 uses them as default separators. This gives equal rhetorical weight to sentences that do not deserve equal weight and makes the draft feel engineered.

### 4. The writer keeps interpreting for the reader

The draft often tells the reader what the example means immediately after the example. Strong explanatory prose can sometimes let two or three connected facts/examples establish the distinction before naming the implication once.

## Approved paragraph-level method for Draft 7

This is **not a paragraph template**. It is a set of constraints derived from the research and approved by the founder/editor on 2026-09-07.

### A. Start the explanation, do not announce it

After the central question and succinct answer, move directly into the weak-sales problem. Do not insert a sentence whose only function is `Here is the important part`.

### B. Stay with one business situation long enough to make it useful

Use weak sales as the main explanatory case. Develop the ambiguity across connected paragraphs:

- what the result shows;
- several plausible causes;
- why different causes imply different responses;
- how the wrong response can worsen the business.

Do not end each of those with a mini-takeaway.

### C. Let paragraph size follow thought size

Combine sentences that belong to one explanatory unit. Use a one-sentence paragraph only when the sentence genuinely deserves emphasis or marks a real turn.

No target paragraph count or sentence count.

### D. State the reader payoff once

The result-versus-cause distinction should be stated clearly, but not repeatedly rephrased throughout the piece.

Once the example has earned the distinction, state it plainly and move to the consequence: the owner is learning while operating, and wrong diagnosis costs resources.

### E. Consequence should grow from the example

Move from weak sales to cost because the previous explanation naturally raises the question: what happens when the owner acts on an uncertain diagnosis?

This keeps the article causal rather than modular.

### F. End at the reader destination

The ending should leave the reader with the useful distinction and its consequence, not another summary of why business is hard.

The final movement should be toward:

- result versus cause;
- diagnosis before resource allocation;
- why effort alone does not solve an unidentified problem.

No motivational instruction and no new topic.

## Explicit anti-slop constraints for Draft 7

Reject the draft if it contains a visible repeated pattern of:

- one-sentence declaration -> example -> one-sentence lesson;
- `This is why...` conclusions after each idea;
- mini-thesis sentences at the start of every paragraph;
- equal-length paragraphs throughout;
- three-item or five-item symmetry created for rhythm;
- paragraphs whose final sentence merely summarizes the paragraph;
- repeated restatement of `result != cause` in different wording;
- transitions that announce structure rather than continue thought.

## Approved recommendation

Keep the approved high-level form:

`question -> succinct answer -> explanation`

For Draft 7, execute the explanation as a **continuous causal development of one concrete business problem**, not as a sequence of outline blocks.

The core movement should be:

`weak result -> ambiguity about cause -> different causes imply different actions -> wrong diagnosis wastes resources -> reader understands why operating and diagnosing happen at the same time`

This is a reasoning dependency, not an instruction to make five sections or five paragraphs.
