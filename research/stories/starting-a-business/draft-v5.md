What actually makes starting a business difficult, beyond simply working long hours?

A new business has to make costly decisions before the owner has enough evidence to know which decisions are right. You are choosing what to sell, what to charge, how to reach customers, and how much money to commit while still learning whether the business works at all.

Weak sales show the problem clearly. If sales are low, the business still does not know why. The price may be wrong. The offer may be unclear. The right customers may not have seen it. Demand may simply be weaker than expected.

Those explanations lead to different decisions. Lowering the price might help if price is the problem, but it can make the economics worse if the real problem is distribution. Spending more on advertising might help if people are not seeing the offer, but it can waste more money if the offer itself is weak.

A bad week tells the owner that sales were weak. It does not tell the owner what caused them to be weak.

So the early work is not only producing and selling. It is also trying to get better information. Talking to customers, changing an offer, trying a different price, or doing something manually before building a larger system can all reveal useful signals. None of those signals settles the question by itself, but they can narrow what the business needs to figure out next.

The difficult part is that this learning has a cost. Inventory can sit unsold. Advertising money can disappear without producing enough customers. Rent, payroll, software, equipment, or the owner's own time can keep adding up while the answer is still unclear.

That is also why persistence is more complicated than simply refusing to quit. More effort can help when the business still has something important to learn or improve. The same effort can keep consuming money when the underlying problem is not changing.

A [2026 randomized study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34755) of 553 science- and technology-based startups found that entrepreneurship training made founders more likely to shut down and to do so sooner. Among ventures that remained open, the trained group also showed stronger later outcomes on several measures. The researchers interpreted the pattern as consistent with founders learning to stop weaker ventures earlier.

That study does not show that closing a business causes success, and it does not represent every kind of business. Its useful point here is narrower: learning can sometimes give a founder a reason to stop, not only a reason to continue.

There is no universal number of months, sales calls, price changes, or failed attempts that tells every business when to keep going. Different businesses have different costs, margins, sales cycles, and alternatives.

The owner still has to decide what to change, what to keep doing, and what to stop before the evidence is complete. That is the part of starting a business that advice about working harder does not explain.
