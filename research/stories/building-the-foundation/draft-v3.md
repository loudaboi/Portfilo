# C027 Draft v3

_Status: INTERNAL DRAFT / NOT PUBLIC_

_Working title: Build things that make the next thing easier_

_Working dek: A project can be worth more than its immediate result if it leaves you in a stronger position for the next attempt._

Two projects can end the same way: they stop.

Neither becomes a company. Neither becomes a career. Neither makes much money.

But the people who built them may not end in the same place.

One project leaves almost nothing behind. It was busy, maybe even impressive while it lasted, but the work cannot be reused, nobody outside the project saw it, and the person who did it learned little that changes the next decision.

The other project also ends. But now the person can make something they could not make before. They understand a problem more clearly. Someone has seen their work. Maybe one customer trusts them. The project is over, but the next attempt does not begin from the same starting point.

We are good at judging the visible outcome. Did it work? Did it make money? Did people use it? Did it become something?

That is usually fair. Outcomes matter.

But when you are early, there is another question worth asking: what did the work leave behind?

Not as consolation for a bad result. As something you can actually use again.

A small product makes this easy to see. Suppose you spend a few months building a tool and only a handful of people use it. By the usual scorecard, it is not much of a success. But if making it forced you to learn how to turn a rough idea into something usable, talk to people with the problem, fix what broke, and put a finished thing in front of strangers, the next product starts from a different place. You are not facing the same unknowns with the same ability.

That does not mean the failed product was secretly successful. It means the outcome is not the only thing that changed.

Paid work can do the same. A small service may never turn into a large business. But doing real work for a real client can teach you where the difficult part actually is, what people are willing to pay for, what they keep asking for, and what you need to get better at. If you decide to build something else later, you are doing it with information you did not have before.

Even work that looks less like a business can change the next starting point. A research project, a newsletter, an event, or a community effort may stay small. Yet if it gives you a body of work people can inspect, introduces you to people you would not have met otherwise, or makes you much better at a useful kind of work, it has altered what you can attempt next.

The useful word here is not learning. It is carryover.

Learning is too easy to claim after the fact. Almost any bad experience can be turned into a lesson if you describe it generously enough. Carryover is stricter. Something has to remain.

Maybe it is a skill you can now use without starting from scratch. Maybe it is proof that makes the next person more willing to take you seriously. Maybe it is a clearer picture of a market, a customer, or a kind of work. Maybe it is money, distribution, reusable code, or a relationship that did not exist before.

You do not need all of these. The point is that the work changes your position.

This matters more when you do not yet have much accumulated leverage.

Someone with years of experience, savings, an audience, strong professional relationships, or a recognized track record already carries things from one project to the next. Their new work does not begin in a vacuum.

Early on, it often does.

You may have to learn the basic skill while doing the work. You may have to convince people to trust you without much evidence. You may not know the industry well enough to see which problems matter. You may not have money or distribution to make mistakes cheaply.

None of that disappears because you choose a good project. But choices can change the next set of choices. If a piece of work leaves you with something usable, your next attempt can begin with a little more capability, a little more evidence, or a little more access than the last one did.

After enough serious attempts, those differences can become significant.

This is also why the size of the project can be misleading. A small thing that changes what you can do next may be more valuable to you than a larger thing that consumes a year and leaves you dependent on the same people, the same skills, and the same opportunities you had before.

That does not mean you should optimize every decision for some future project. At some point, you have to commit to the thing in front of you. Real work usually gets interesting only after the novelty wears off.

The idea is not to treat everything as a stepping stone.

It is to notice whether the work is building any durable advantage while you do it.

There is a danger here, because this argument can become a perfect excuse for wasting time.

A project fails, so you say you learned a lot. Another project never ships, but it helped you explore. Six months disappear into a third attempt, and now you tell yourself the network was worth it.

That story can go on forever.

Residual value is not something you get to declare just because the main outcome was disappointing. If the work changed nothing specific, there may be nothing to celebrate. Repeating the same mistakes is not compounding. Collecting unfinished projects is not a strategy.

The test has to be concrete.

Could you now do something you could not do before? Is there finished work another person can see? Do you understand the problem well enough to make a better decision next time? Did the work create an actual relationship, customer, capability, asset, or source of income that continues after the project ends?

If the answer is no, then `it was valuable anyway` may just be a nicer way to describe sunk time.

But when the answer is yes, it changes how an early project should be judged.

The best outcome is still for the thing itself to work. Build the product people want. Make the business viable. Do the job well. Grow the project if it deserves to grow.

The point is not to lower the standard for success.

It is to avoid making every early attempt carry the burden of becoming your final answer.

Some things are worth building because they work. Some are worth building because they change what becomes possible afterward. The strongest early work can do both.

So when you are choosing what to spend months on, there is one question worth adding to the obvious ones:

**If this does not become the thing, what will it make easier to do next?**

A good answer will not guarantee that the project works.

It may still mean that when the project ends, you do not go back to the beginning.

Your next attempt starts from somewhere new.
