## Human-edited framing sample

This pass reviewed a broader set of headlines from established edited publications. The purpose was to identify **headline patterns**, not to copy wording.

### 1. The Atlantic — `Workism Is Making Americans Miserable`

Pattern: direct thesis / consequence.

Lesson: a Perspective can state its argument plainly in the headline and leave qualification to the deck/body.

### 2. The Atlantic — `Think Twice Before Taking the Top Job`

Pattern: imperative caution.

Lesson: an imperative headline does not automatically mean the piece is a step-by-step Guide. It can carry the thesis of an argument.

### 3. The Atlantic — `The Upside of Professional Rejection`

Pattern: tension/value noun phrase.

Lesson: a headline can name the disputed category without making a universal claim.

### 4. The Atlantic — `What Does Life After Ambition Actually Look Like?`

Pattern: genuine question.

Lesson: question headlines fit when the article is genuinely exploring uncertainty. C027 already has a settled argument, so this pattern is less appropriate.

### 5. Harvard Business Review — `When to Stick with Something — and When to Quit`

Pattern: decision frame.

Lesson: explicit decision language is useful when the article helps readers choose between alternatives. C027 is interpretive rather than a direct decision guide, so this pattern would overstate application.

### 6. Harvard Business Review — `Write a Failure Résumé to Learn What Makes You Succeed`

Pattern: imperative + promised benefit.

Lesson: imperative construction is common in edited business writing. The risk is not the grammar itself; the risk is promising more than the article supports.

### 7. Harvard Business Review — `When Your Job Is Your Identity, Professional Failure Hurts More`

Pattern: conditional consequence.

Lesson: conditional language can narrow a claim in the headline itself when the qualifier is central.

### 8. Harvard Business Review — `Quitting Can Sometimes Be a Strategic Success`

Pattern: bounded direct claim.

Lesson: words such as `can` and `sometimes` are useful when the central idea is contingent rather than universal.

### 9. WIRED — `Did Assignment Zero Fail? A Look Back, and Lessons Learned`

Pattern: question + scope/retrospective frame.

Lesson: question framing works when the story's job is to evaluate a specific case. C027 is broader and should not pretend there is an unresolved reported case.

### 10. WIRED — `The Era of the #fumblebrag`

Pattern: coined/clever framing.

Lesson: a distinctive coined label can carry a magazine story, but C027's approved style research explicitly rejects inventing a framework term merely to create headline personality.

## Cross-sample findings

### Finding 1 — imperative headlines are legitimate for arguments

The first framing pass treated the imperative form of `Build things that make the next thing easier` as evidence of a content-type mismatch.

The larger sample does **not** support that as a general rule.

Edited Perspectives/advice-analysis pieces use imperatives when the imperative itself states the argument.

Therefore:

> imperative grammar alone is not a reason to reject the existing C027 headline.

### Finding 2 — strong headlines usually carry one proposition, not every caveat

The examples generally do not encode the full counterargument, evidence standard, limitation, and conclusion in one title.

The headline establishes the main proposition/tension. Qualification can move into the dek.

Implication for C027:

Do not force the headline to explain the `I learned a lot` failure mode or every condition under which later value counts.

### Finding 3 — failure should not become the subject unless the whole article is about failure

The rejected first package centered `failed project` because failure is the hardest test of the argument.

That was an editorial overcorrection.

The approved body also covers work that simply ends, remains small, or does not become continuing work.

Implication:

A final headline should keep **work/project choice and what comes next** as the subject, not failure alone.

### Finding 4 — boundedness can be carried by the dek

Human-edited headline/dek systems commonly use the secondary line to add scope or qualification.

Implication:

C027's headline may remain concise/memorable if the dek immediately states that direct outcome still matters and that later value requires a concrete change.

### Finding 5 — question headlines are unnecessary here

C027 already has a position.

A question headline would create false uncertainty or make the story sound like an explainer.

### Finding 6 — clever coinage is not needed

The approved body needs no framework term. The framing should not add one.

## Re-audit of the existing headline

### `Build things that make the next thing easier`

Pattern:

imperative thesis.

### Strengths after deeper comparison

- states the article's actual normative argument rather than a side issue;
- keeps the subject broader than failure;
- uses ordinary words;
- memorable without needing a coined framework;
- points toward future usefulness, which is the approved body's central concern;
- already has explicit human editorial history rather than being a new AI-generated phrase introduced during this pass.

### Risks

- `easier` is broader than the body's strict standard; a project may improve a later decision without literally making every part of the next attempt easier;
- imperative tone makes the piece more directive than a purely descriptive Perspective;
- isolated without a dek, it could be read as general motivational advice.

### Can the dek solve those risks?

Yes, if the dek immediately establishes:

1. result first;
2. later usefulness only counts when the work changes the next attempt in a concrete way.

The larger framing sample supports using the dek for that qualification instead of discarding an otherwise accurate/memorable headline.

## Headline decision

**RECOMMEND RETAINING THE EXISTING HEADLINE.**

> **Build things that make the next thing easier**

This reverses the first framing recommendation.

Reason:

The previous pass over-weighted the imperative-form concern and replaced the broad project-choice argument with a narrower failure headline. The broader human-edited comparison does not justify that trade.

## Dek job

The dek must prevent the headline from becoming generic motivational advice.

It should establish the article's stricter standard in plain language without trying to reproduce the whole counterargument.

### Proposed dek

> **The result still comes first. Beyond that, the work should only count if it changes how the next attempt begins in a concrete way.**

Why this pattern fits:

- headline = main proposition;
- dek = scope/qualification;
- two sentences rather than a slogan;
- does not center failure;
- does not invent a framework term;
- does not copy a comparator's phrasing.

Potential issue:

`should only count` is explicitly normative. That is acceptable for a Perspective but remains subject to Louda's editorial judgment.

## Description job

The description should be literal and useful in cards/search/social metadata.

It should not try to sound like a second headline.

### Proposed description

> **A Perspective on judging early projects by their result and by whether the work changes how a later attempt begins.**

Why this pattern fits:

- page-specific;
- literal;
- identifies the content type;
- no promotional claim;
- no headline repetition;
- summarizes the article's two-part judgment without importing the failure framing.

## Alternative headline patterns considered after deeper research

### `What early work leaves behind`

Pattern: noun/question-adjacent concept title.

Decision: reject.

Reason: too vague; it does not state the argument and could describe many kinds of retrospective essay.

### `A project can change what comes next`

Pattern: bounded direct claim.

Decision: hold only.

Reason: accurate but generic; loses the decision/action energy and distinctiveness of the original headline.

### `The next attempt starts with what the last one changed`

Pattern: declarative thesis.

Decision: reject.

Reason: more abstract and more obviously generated; less natural than the existing headline.

The comparison establishes **patterns and editorial functions**, not wording authority.
