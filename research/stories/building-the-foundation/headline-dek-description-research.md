## Purpose

Research the three framing jobs separately after the body was approved:

- headline: name the argument accurately and specifically;
- dek: add the qualification/tension the headline cannot carry;
- description: provide a compact literal summary for cards, indexes, SEO, and social metadata.

This pass does not authorize replacing the live story.

## Professional guidance

### Poynter — stronger headlines

Source:

https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2017/9-tips-for-writing-stronger-headlines/

Relevant guidance used:

- be specific rather than vague;
- do not mislead or overpromise;
- start from the actual subject/action;
- write for the reader rather than to display cleverness.

### Poynter — headline checklist

Source:

https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2017/10-questions-for-your-headline-writing-checklist/

Relevant guidance used:

- every word must be accurate;
- headline should make the story's real point, not a side issue or entry device;
- do not undersell or oversell;
- it should be easy to understand and match the story's tone.

### Google Search Central — meta descriptions

Source:

https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet

Relevant guidance used:

- the description should be a short, relevant, page-specific summary;
- it should accurately describe the page rather than contain generic marketing language;
- there is no fixed required character count; Google may truncate snippets based on device/context;
- the meta description may be used when it describes the page better than other page content.

## Headline audit

### Existing headline

`Build things that make the next thing easier`

Strengths:

- memorable;
- close to the original editorial idea;
- concrete words;
- accurately points toward later usefulness.

Weaknesses:

- imperative construction makes the story sound more like a Guide or motivational instruction than the approved Perspective body;
- `make the next thing easier` is broader than the body, which uses a stricter test: the work must change a later choice or create something usable;
- it risks sounding like a slogan when isolated on a homepage card.

Decision:

`DO NOT RECOMMEND AS FINAL` unless Louda explicitly prefers retaining it despite the content-type mismatch.

### Candidate A — recommended

`A failed project can still change what comes next`

Why it works:

- declarative rather than instructional;
- makes the argumentative tension visible immediately;
- `can` preserves contingency rather than promising that failure is useful;
- easy to parse;
- matches the body's main example and counterargument.

Risk:

The body also uses a one-off client engagement that is not necessarily a failure, so this headline emphasizes the failure side of the argument more than the whole essay.

### Candidate B

`What a project leaves behind`

Strengths:

- broad enough to cover both failed products and completed client work;
- non-instructional;
- concise.

Weaknesses:

- too vague by Poynter's specificity test;
- does not state the argument or qualification;
- could describe a retrospective essay, a project-management article, or a memorial piece.

Decision:

`REJECT`.

### Candidate C

`The outcome is not the only thing a project changes`

Strengths:

- directly states the core argument;
- covers both examples;
- avoids Guide framing.

Weaknesses:

- slightly abstract;
- `not the only thing` construction has the symmetrical/AI-shaped feel already identified as a risk in C027;
- less natural as a headline.

Decision:

`HOLD`.

### Candidate D

`A project can change the next decision even when it ends`

Strengths:

- close to the body's actual standard;
- covers short/ended work without labeling all of it failure;
- declarative and bounded.

Weaknesses:

- somewhat mechanical;
- `even when it ends` is imprecise because all projects eventually end and the body is really about disappointing/limited direct outcomes.

Decision:

`HOLD`.

## Headline recommendation

Preferred:

> **A failed project can still change what comes next**

Reason:

It best balances specificity, readability, content-type fit, and the article's central tension. The risk of over-centering failure can be corrected by the dek, which should state that the direct result still comes first and make clear that additional value requires something concrete to change.

## Dek research and recommendation

Job:

- qualify the headline;
- state the stricter standard;
- avoid repeating the headline;
- avoid sounding like a motivational promise.

Proposed dek:

> **The direct result still comes first. Work deserves additional credit only when it changes a later decision or leaves something that can actually be used.**

Audit:

- adds qualification rather than repeating the headline: PASS;
- makes the article's limiting principle visible: PASS;
- no Portfilo institutional claim: PASS;
- no rhetorical question: PASS;
- no metaphor/framework term: PASS;
- does not imply every failure is useful: PASS.

Potential concern:

`additional credit` is evaluative language. It is justified by the article's actual argument, but should not be repeated as a branded concept inside the body.

## Description research and recommendation

Job:

- literal, page-specific summary;
- useful on cards, archive, SEO, and social metadata;
- different rhetorical job from the visible dek;
- no fixed character target invented.

Proposed description:

> **A Perspective on judging early projects by their direct outcome and whether the work changes a later choice or leaves something usable.**

Audit:

- page-specific: PASS;
- literal: PASS;
- distinguishes content type: PASS;
- summarizes the article without rhetorical setup: PASS;
- unique from dek: PASS;
- no unsupported quality/promotion claim: PASS.

Alternative without content-type label:

> **Early projects can be judged by their direct outcome and, secondarily, by whether the work changes a later choice or leaves something usable.**

Assessment:

This is slightly more natural, but `can be judged` reads as an unattributed normative assertion in metadata. Because the page already labels the content type, either version is defensible. The first is more explicit about the piece being a Perspective.
