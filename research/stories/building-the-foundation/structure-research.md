# C027 Structure Research

_Last updated: 2026-09-03_

_Status: STRUCTURE RESEARCH COMPLETE / NO DRAFT AUTHORIZED_

## Approved core idea

> **Build things that make the next thing easier.**

Working interpretation:

> When you are early, the immediate outcome is not the only way to judge work. Some projects leave behind reusable value that improves the next starting point: knowledge, skill, proof, relationships, money, distribution, judgment, or a clearer understanding of a problem.

The article must not turn that list into a framework. Those are examples of what may remain after the work. The story has one idea: **good early work can improve the position from which the next attempt begins.**

## Craft findings

### 1. One throughline must govern inclusion

Longreads' essay guidance accepts many forms but repeatedly requires one clear thread and an arc that begins close to the top. Its current submission guidance also asks writers to identify the shape of the essay, the concern powering it, the evidence used, and the conclusion the reader should leave with.

Sources:
- https://longreads.com/2017/08/18/longreads-essays-editor-sari-bottons-guide-to-pitching/
- https://longreads.com/submissions/

Implication for C027:

The draft should not contain separate mini-essays on skills, networking, failure, AI, reputation, habits, or entrepreneurship. A detail stays only if it demonstrates how one piece of work can alter the next starting point.

### 2. Theme should function as a filter, not decoration

Nieman Storyboard's structure guidance treats theme as the throughline that helps decide which facts, scenes, and details belong. Jack Hart's theme-statement method similarly uses one proposition to guide structure and determine what to leave out.

Sources:
- https://niemanstoryboard.org/2022/02/02/sticking-a-story-together-and-nailing-the-structure/
- https://niemanstoryboard.org/2021/10/19/nut-grafs-getting-to-the-heart-of-the-story/

Internal C027 theme statement:

> **Some work matters because it changes the quality of the next starting point.**

This sentence is an editorial control, not proposed public copy.

### 3. A funnel suits this idea better than a list

Walt Harrington describes a strong story as a funnel that raises a question and narrows toward insight. That is more suitable for C027 than a numbered framework because the article is a Perspective, not a Guide.

Source:
- https://niemanstoryboard.org/1997/03/28/a-writers-essay-seeking-the-extraordinary-in-the-ordinary-2/

Implication for C027:

Begin with an intuitive way people judge work, introduce the missing dimension, follow its consequences, test the idea against its obvious abuse case, and end with a sharper way to judge an early project.

### 4. Paul Graham's useful lesson is argumentative progression, not voice imitation

`Do Things that Don't Scale`, `Before the Startup`, and `How to Do Great Work` tend to establish an assumption or problem early, state a counterintuitive reframe, and then follow its implications through examples and exceptions. The structure remains visible enough to guide the reader but does not feel like a formula.

Sources:
- https://www.paulgraham.com/ds.html
- https://www.paulgraham.com/before.html
- https://www.paulgraham.com/greatwork.html

A particularly relevant structural principle from `How to Do Great Work` is that uncertain early choices can be judged partly by whether they preserve or improve future options. C027 should not copy Graham's terminology or argument, but the essay demonstrates how one idea can expand naturally without becoming several separate topics.

### 5. Concrete contrast can carry the abstraction

The message `improve the next starting point` is abstract. A strong opening therefore needs a concrete contrast rather than a definition.

The most useful device is two pieces of work with similar visible outcomes but different residual value. The contrast can be hypothetical and ordinary rather than dramatic.

Example of the structural function, not proposed prose:

- one project ends and leaves almost nothing reusable;
- another project also ends, but the person now knows a market, has proof of finished work, understands customers better, or has a relationship that changes the next attempt.

The essay then asks why we tend to judge both projects only by the visible outcome.

### 6. The counterargument is essential

Without a counterargument, `the project left me with something` can become a flattering excuse for wasted effort, unfinished work, or endless project-hopping.

The article therefore needs one explicit turn:

> Residual value is not a consolation prize you can assign after anything fails.

The draft should distinguish between work that genuinely leaves transferable value and work where `learning` is only a story told afterward.

This keeps the Perspective from becoming motivational content.

## Structure candidates considered

### A. Contrast-led argumentative funnel

Shape:

1. Show two efforts with similar immediate outcomes but different next starting points.
2. Surface the hidden variable: what remains after the project.
3. Develop the idea through a small number of concrete examples.
4. Show why this matters most when someone is early and has little accumulated leverage.
5. Introduce the abuse case: not every failed or unfinished project becomes valuable because we call it learning.
6. Give the reader a better test for choosing/judging work.
7. End by returning to the starting-position idea.

Strengths:
- immediately concrete;
- one clear thread;
- reader-centered;
- allows breadth without a listicle;
- naturally supports a counterargument;
- closest to the selected message.

Risk:
- the opening contrast could feel contrived if written too neatly;
- examples can accidentally become a list of assets.

Decision:
**SELECTED.**

### B. Single-project progression

Shape:
Follow one hypothetical project from beginning to end, showing how its visible outcome may be modest while its residual value accumulates.

Strengths:
- easy to follow;
- naturally narrative;
- concrete.

Risk:
- a hypothetical protagonist can feel artificial;
- one example may narrow a broad Perspective toward startups or software;
- the reader may focus on whether the example is realistic rather than the idea.

Decision:
`HOLD / POSSIBLE DEVICE INSIDE A.`

### C. Question-led philosophical essay

Shape:
Open with `What is a project for?`, challenge the default answer, then develop the distinction between outcome value and residual value.

Strengths:
- elegant;
- closest to classic idea-essay form;
- low structural overhead.

Risk:
- too abstract for the first featured Perspective;
- high risk of reproducing the problem in Draft v1, where the reader had to reconstruct the message.

Decision:
`REJECT AS PRIMARY STRUCTURE.`

### D. Personal observation -> universal argument

Shape:
Louda briefly describes noticing that current work is changing what becomes possible next, then broadens the point to the reader.

Strengths:
- gives the byline a visible reason to be personal;
- can feel human and grounded.

Risk:
- shifts attention toward the author;
- can become a founder post or imply a success story that does not exist yet;
- unnecessary because the idea can stand without biography.

Decision:
`REJECT FOR C027.`

## Selected structure

Use **A: Contrast-led argumentative funnel**.

### Beat 1: The visible outcome

Open with a familiar judgment: a project succeeded, failed, made money, got users, became a job, or went nowhere.

Then introduce a contrast in which two apparently similar outcomes leave people in different positions afterward.

Purpose:
Create curiosity before explaining the thesis.

### Beat 2: What remains

Name the overlooked dimension plainly: some work leaves behind something that can be used again.

Do not immediately enumerate everything that can remain.

Purpose:
Deliver the reframe early enough that the reader knows what the essay is about.

### Beat 3: Show the idea moving through ordinary examples

Use two or three different kinds of work so the idea is not startup-only. Possible domains:

- a small product/project;
- paid work or a service;
- publishing/research/community work.

Each example must perform the same job: demonstrate a changed next starting point.

Purpose:
Make the abstraction tangible without converting it into `7 assets to build`.

### Beat 4: Why this matters more when you are early

Someone with established capital, distribution, expertise, or reputation already has accumulated leverage. Someone early often does not.

The point is not that structural disadvantages disappear. The point is that, when choices are available, work that leaves reusable value can gradually change what becomes possible next.

Purpose:
Connect the idea to the intended early audience without flattering them or narrating Louda's biography.

### Beat 5: The hard counterargument

Challenge the idea directly.

A dead project is not automatically a good project. Repetition can be stagnation. `I learned a lot` can be a rationalization. Some work really does waste time.

The relevant question is whether something specific and usable changed.

Purpose:
Prevent motivational drift and make the argument credible.

### Beat 6: A better test

Offer one question rather than a framework:

> **If this does not become the thing, what will it make easier to do next?**

This is currently a working conceptual question, not an approved public line.

Purpose:
Give the reader a memorable way to apply the Perspective.

### Beat 7: Resolution

Return to the starting-position concept.

The essay should end on the idea that progress early on can be visible not only in what has been built, but in the fact that the next attempt no longer begins from the same place.

Do not add a motivational crescendo, a list, or an `anything is possible` conclusion.

## Length and presentation recommendation

Target Draft v3 length:

**1,100-1,500 words.**

Reason:
The selected idea needs enough room for contrast, development and counterargument, but does not justify a 2,500+ word long-form treatment. The previous drafts became weaker when additional concepts were added to justify length.

Recommended presentation:

- mostly continuous prose;
- at most two or three quiet subheads if the site needs visual breathing room;
- no numbered list;
- no methodology box;
- citations only if factual claims are introduced;
- no forced AI paragraph;
- no author biography inside the body;
- no `grind`, `hustle`, or `starting from nothing` framing;
- no invented anecdote presented as fact.
