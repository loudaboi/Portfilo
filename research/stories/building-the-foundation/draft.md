_Working title: What do you build when you're not ready yet?_

_Working dek: AI can lower some knowledge and technical barriers. It does not remove the need to build judgment, skills, habits, relationships, trust, and an understanding of what people actually want._

There is a version of building advice that starts too late.

It begins with the product. Find a problem. Build something. Launch it. Get users. Improve it.

Useful advice, if you already have enough of the things required to act on it.

But a lot of people start earlier than that.

They have the ambition before they have the money. The idea before they have the technical ability. The interest before they know the right people. They can see a direction they want to move in without yet being the person who can execute all of it.

That can look like one giant problem: **I'm not ready.**

A better way to look at it is as a series of smaller ones.

## The bottleneck keeps moving

Suppose you want to build a product but cannot make even a rough version of it.

For now, that is the bottleneck.

You do not need to become an expert in everything. You need enough knowledge, skill, help, or tooling to get past that constraint and see the next one.

Maybe you eventually make the prototype.

Now the technical barrier is smaller, but another problem appears: you do not know whether anybody actually wants what you made.

So the work changes.

You talk to people. Watch how they solve the problem now. Learn what they care about. Find out which part of your idea sounded important only because you were the person who thought of it.

Then the bottleneck might move again.

Perhaps you know what needs to be made, but you are inconsistent. Or people have no reason to trust you yet. Or you know capable people but have not built real relationships with them. Or you can make something useful but have no way to get it in front of anyone.

There is no universal order here. The point is that the constraint changes.

Treating the whole distance between where you are and where you want to be as one problem makes the path look impossible. Treating the next constraint as a problem you can work on makes it concrete.

## AI changes some of the starting conditions

AI makes this more interesting because some barriers that used to stop people earlier can now be crossed sooner.

There is evidence for that, although it is much narrower than the claim that AI suddenly makes everyone equally capable.

A [2026 randomized experiment](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851) had 1,174 adults complete a workplace-style business problem-solving task with or without a generative-AI assistant. Everyone improved with AI, but the gains were substantially larger among participants with less education. In that specific task, the performance gap between the two education groups shrank by about three-quarters while AI was available.

Earlier [workplace research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) found something similar in customer support: an AI assistant raised productivity most for novice and lower-skilled workers.

That does not mean expertise stopped mattering.

In the 2026 experiment, a substantial education-linked gap returned when participants later worked without AI. And AI does not make every expert faster either. A [randomized study by METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) found that experienced open-source developers working in codebases they knew well actually took longer with the early-2025 AI tools used in the experiment.

The useful conclusion is smaller.

AI can compress some gaps in some kinds of work. It can let someone attempt a task earlier, produce a rough version faster, get explanations on demand, or work through technical steps they could not have handled alone.

That is real leverage.

But leverage is not the same thing as having built the underlying capability.

## When one barrier gets cheaper, another becomes visible

If making a first version becomes easier, deciding what deserves to be made matters more.

If information becomes easier to access, knowing what to believe matters more.

If code becomes easier to produce, understanding whether it is correct matters more.

If everyone can produce more, earning attention and trust does not automatically become easier.

This is why the answer is not simply to use AI for everything and move faster.

A 2026 [meta-analysis of studies on AI coding assistants](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04779) found a moderate productivity benefit overall, but no statistically significant improvement in measured programming-learning outcomes. Faster assisted output and durable skill are not necessarily the same thing.

There are parts of the foundation you still have to build yourself.

Judgment comes from making decisions and seeing what happens. Understanding people comes from paying attention to them. Trust comes from what you repeatedly do. Relationships take interaction over time. Good habits matter because tools are much less useful if nothing gets finished.

AI can help with all of those. It cannot simply hand them to you.

## Building the foundation is still building

The danger is turning this idea into endless preparation.

You can spend years telling yourself you are building skills, building a network, building knowledge, getting ready.

At some point, preparation becomes another way of avoiding the work.

The foundation has to connect to something real.

Learn because you need to make the next version. Meet people because there are things you can build, learn, or contribute together. Improve the way you work because your current habits are preventing you from finishing. Build a reputation by doing work that gives people a reason to trust you.

Each part should remove a real constraint.

That also means the first thing you try does not have to become the thing that works.

A product can fail while leaving you better at making products. A project can go nowhere while introducing you to people you later build with. Learning a technical skill for one idea can make the next idea possible. Doing useful work in public can create trust before you know exactly where that trust will matter.

The visible thing might fail while the foundation keeps accumulating.

That is a less dramatic way to think about building than the usual story of one idea, one breakthrough, one company.

It is also more useful when you are early.

You do not need to pretend the distance is small. You do not need to begin with all the money, knowledge, skills, connections, or credibility you may eventually need.

You need to figure out what is stopping the next useful step.

Then build enough to take it.
