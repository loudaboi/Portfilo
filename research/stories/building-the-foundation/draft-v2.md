_Working title: Your first project does not have to be your future_

_Working dek: An early project can be valuable even if it never becomes a company, career, or identity. Its job may be simpler: teach you something real about what to do next._

One of the easiest ways to get stuck early is to think your first serious project has to be the right one.

The right business. The right career move. The right idea to spend the next five years on.

That is a lot to ask from someone who is still learning what they are good at, what people care about, and what kind of work they can stay interested in long enough to become useful.

So they wait. They read. They plan. They try to become ready for a decision that experience is supposed to help them make.

A better first project may be one that gives you better information.

It can be small: a service for one customer, a tool used by ten people, a newsletter with twenty readers, an event, a tiny shop, a research project, a piece of software. What matters is that it becomes real enough for the world to push back.

Before that point, almost everything is theory.

You think people have a problem. You think you can solve it. You think you would enjoy the work. You think you need a certain skill. You think the idea is clear.

Then you make something and reality starts editing the plan.

Maybe nobody cares about the part you spent the most time on. Maybe the small feature you nearly removed is the only thing people mention. Maybe selling is harder than building. Maybe you discover that you like talking to customers more than making the thing, or the reverse. Maybe someone you meet through the project becomes more important to your future than the project itself.

None of this guarantees success. That is not the point.

The point is that the project has turned unknowns into things you can actually respond to.

This is why it can be a mistake to judge every early project only by whether it `worked`.

If `worked` means it became a large business, a long-term career, or the thing you are eventually known for, most first attempts will look disappointing.

But an early project can leave you in a different place even when the project itself ends.

You may understand the problem better. You may know what kind of work you are willing to keep doing. You may have finished something you can show. You may have met people because there was finally something concrete to talk about. Most importantly, the next decision is no longer being made from the same starting point.

That does not make failure automatically valuable.

There is an obvious trap in telling yourself that every unfinished side project is `learning`.

A project only teaches much when it becomes real enough to create consequences. You have to finish enough of it to show somebody. You have to let people ignore it, criticize it, use it, reject it, or pay for it. You have to stay with it long enough to find out which of your assumptions survive contact with another person.

Otherwise, `building to learn` can become preparation with better branding.

The goal is not to keep hopping between projects forever. It is for each serious attempt to reduce some of the uncertainty around the next one.

Sometimes the first project will become the thing. Great.

But it does not need to carry that pressure from the beginning.

When you are early, choosing your entire future may be the wrong problem. A smaller and more useful question is whether the thing you are building will teach you something you could not have learned by thinking about it.

You do not need your first project to answer everything.

You need it to tell you something true about what to do next.
