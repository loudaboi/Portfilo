Suppose two small software products each attract a few users, fail to keep them, and shut down after a few months. The obvious verdict is the same: neither product worked.

That verdict matters. A project should not be promoted into a success just because it was difficult or educational. But the outcome of the project is not always the only thing the work changes.

One builder may finish with little they can use again. Another may have released something for the first time, watched users struggle with it, and discovered that the problem they expected people to care about was not urgent enough. The next time they consider an idea, they test the problem earlier instead of spending months building first. They also have finished work another person can inspect.

The failed product remains a failed product. The difference is in what the work has changed outside it.

For someone without much previous work to draw on, that difference can be worth noticing. The direct result still comes first: did the work solve the problem, earn money, attract users, or deserve to continue? Alongside that result, an early project can sometimes create something concrete that a later attempt can use.

A one-off client job shows the same distinction in a different setting. Imagine someone is paid to automate a repetitive reporting task for a small business. The job ends after delivery and never becomes an agency or long contract. During the work, however, the client keeps returning to one costly step in the process and is willing to pay specifically to remove it. The next time the person looks for similar work, they can describe that problem precisely and show that they have already solved a version of it for someone who paid.

The useful part is not a vague claim that client work builds experience. The job changed the next sales conversation. It supplied evidence about a problem, a buyer, and a piece of work that can be shown.

Previous work already does this for people with a track record. A new project can begin with examples, referrals, technical experience, customer knowledge, or trust earned somewhere else. Someone with little history has fewer things to reuse. A single finished project can add one, even when the project itself stays small.

Project choice cannot erase the conditions around that work. Money, health, family responsibilities, access, timing, and luck can determine which opportunities are available at all. The argument only applies inside the choices a person actually has. Where there is a choice, it is reasonable to consider whether one option is more likely than another to leave something usable if its main goal falls short.

The argument becomes useless, however, if every failed project qualifies. Almost anything can be redescribed afterward as a learning experience. Months can disappear into unfinished work, repeated mistakes, or problems nobody wanted solved, followed by a generous story about personal growth.

A stronger test is whether the work changed a later action or created evidence that did not exist before. If the next decision is no different, there is nothing credible to show, and no relationship, capability, or understanding from the project can be used again, then `I learned a lot` is probably doing more work than the project did.

This is also why the secondary value of a project is more useful to consider before committing to it than after it fails. A person can ask in advance what, specifically, could remain useful even if the main outcome disappoints. The answer should be concrete enough to affect the decision. `Experience` by itself is too vague. A finished piece of work that can be inspected, evidence that changes which problem to pursue, or a relationship created through actual delivery is easier to evaluate.

None of those outcomes guarantees that the project is worth doing. A bad main bet does not become good because it has a plausible side benefit. The exercise simply makes the tradeoff harder to rewrite after the fact.

The direct outcome deserves an honest verdict. So does whatever the work actually changed. If a project ends, the useful question is whether the next decision is different for a reason the work itself created. If it is not, there may be very little to carry forward.
