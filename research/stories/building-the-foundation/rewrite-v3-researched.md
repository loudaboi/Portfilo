Imagine two people each spend several months on a small software product. Both release it, attract a few users, and shut it down when those users do not return. Measured against the product's goal, both projects failed.

The work can still leave the two people in different positions afterward. One may have spent most of the project building before showing it to anyone. The other may have discovered, through actual use, that the problem was less urgent than expected and that the next idea should be tested before another long build. The second person also has finished work that can be inspected rather than a plan that only existed privately.

None of that changes the verdict on the product. It did not work. But an early project can affect more than its own outcome. It can also create something specific that changes how a later attempt begins.

That distinction is useful because early work is often judged in all-or-nothing terms. A product becomes a business or it does not. A side project attracts an audience or disappears. A piece of work turns into a continuing opportunity or ends. Those outcomes deserve an honest reading. The additional question is narrower: after the outcome is known, did the work itself change a later decision, capability, or piece of evidence in a way that can actually be used?

Consider the failed software product again. Discovering that people will not keep using it is only useful if that information changes what happens next. The builder might test demand before committing months to the next product, or might avoid a problem that looked interesting but proved unimportant to the people who tried the first version. A finished product can also be shown to a future collaborator or employer in a way that an unfinished idea cannot. The project has not become successful; it has changed the evidence available for the next decision.

The same logic can apply outside software. Suppose someone takes a one-off client job to automate a repetitive reporting task. The engagement ends after delivery and never becomes a larger contract. During the work, the client repeatedly focuses on one costly step and is willing to pay to remove it. Later, the person can approach similar work with a clearer description of the problem and a finished example of having solved a version of it for a paying customer. The short contract remains short, but it has changed the next conversation in a concrete way.

This argument has limits because people do not begin with the same set of choices. Money, health, family responsibilities, access, timing, and luck can determine which projects are possible in the first place. A better choice among available projects does not remove those constraints. The claim is only that, where there is a genuine choice, the likely effect on future work can be one factor in deciding which effort is worth the cost.

There is also an obvious way to abuse the idea. Once a project fails, almost any amount of time can be defended by saying that something was learned. An unfinished product becomes "experience." A repeated mistake becomes "growth." A project nobody wanted becomes valuable because it supposedly taught a lesson.

That standard is too loose. Spending time on something guarantees experience only in the literal sense that time was spent doing it. Useful change requires more. A later action should be different because of what happened, or there should be something new that can be shown, used, or tested. If the supposed lesson does not alter a decision and leaves nothing that can be carried into later work, it does not deserve much weight in judging the project.

This makes the secondary value of a project more useful before the work begins than after it fails. It can be described in terms that are specific enough to check later. A weak answer is that the project will provide experience. A stronger answer identifies what would exist even if the main goal falls short: a finished piece of work that can be inspected, evidence that rules out or sharpens a problem, or a relationship created through actual delivery. Those possibilities do not rescue a bad main bet, but they make the tradeoff harder to rewrite after the fact.

The direct outcome remains the main verdict. Secondary value counts only when the work itself changes a later choice or creates something that can actually be used again. Otherwise there is no reason to give a failed or unfinished project extra credit simply because a lesson can be invented afterward.
