Imagine two people each spend several months on a small software product. Both release it, attract a few users, and shut it down when those users do not return. Measured against the product's goal, both projects failed.

The work can still leave the two people in different positions afterward. One may have spent most of the project building before showing it to anyone. The other may have discovered, through actual use, that the problem was less urgent than expected and that the next idea should be tested before another long build. The second person also has finished work that can be inspected rather than a plan that only existed privately.

None of that changes the verdict on the product. It did not work. An early project can still affect more than its own outcome by creating something specific that changes how a later attempt begins.

The direct result deserves the first judgment: the product became a business or it did not; the work continued or it ended. A separate consideration is whether the project changed a later decision or left behind something that can actually be used. That claim has to be concrete enough to survive an honest look at what happened.

Consider the failed software product again. Discovering that people will not keep using it is useful only if that information changes what happens next. The builder might test demand before committing months to the next product, or avoid returning to a problem that looked interesting but proved unimportant to the people who tried the first version. A finished product can also be shown to a future collaborator or employer in a way that an unfinished idea cannot. The failure remains; the available evidence has changed.

The same logic can apply outside software. Suppose someone takes a one-off client job to automate a repetitive reporting task. The engagement ends after delivery and never becomes a larger contract. During the work, the client repeatedly focuses on one costly step and is willing to pay to remove it. Later, the person can approach similar work with a clearer description of the problem and a finished example of having solved a version of it for a paying customer. The short contract has changed the next conversation in a concrete way even though it did not become continuing work.

The argument has limits because people do not begin with the same set of choices. Money, health, family responsibilities, access, timing, and luck can determine which projects are possible in the first place. Choosing well among available options cannot remove those constraints. Where a genuine choice exists, however, the likely effect on later work can reasonably be one factor in deciding whether an effort is worth its cost.

The idea becomes weak when every failed project qualifies. Once something ends badly, it is easy to search backward for a lesson that makes the time sound useful. An unfinished product can be called experience even when the next attempt repeats the same decisions.

Spending time on something guarantees experience only in the literal sense that time was spent doing it. Useful change requires a higher standard. A later action should be different because of what happened, or the work should have produced something that can be shown, used, or tested. If neither is true, a retrospective lesson should not add much weight to the project's value.

That standard is more useful before the work begins than after it fails. Any value beyond the main outcome should be specific enough to check later. A finished piece of work that another person can inspect is one example. Evidence that changes which problem deserves another attempt is another. `Experience` by itself is too vague to make the tradeoff clear in advance.

The direct outcome remains the main verdict. A failed project deserves credit beyond that result only when the work itself changes a later choice or produces something that can actually be used again. Otherwise there is no reason to improve the verdict simply because a lesson can be invented afterward.
