# C027 rewrite v1

_Status: INTERNAL DRAFT / NOT APPROVED FOR PUBLICATION_

Working headline candidate: **Build things that make the next thing easier**

Two small products can end with roughly the same result. Neither becomes a business. Both attract a few users, stall, and are eventually shut down. But the people who built them do not necessarily leave with the same starting position.

In one case, the project leaves little that can be used again. In the other, the builder has finished and released something, watched where real users struggled, and learned enough about the problem to make a different decision next time. The direct outcome is still disappointing. The useful difference is that the next attempt does not begin with exactly the same uncertainty.

A project should first be judged by what happened to the project. Did it solve a real problem? Did anyone use it? Did anyone pay? Was it worth continuing? Those questions should not be softened just because the work was difficult.

But when someone does not yet have much previous work to draw on, there is another reasonable question: did the project leave behind anything specific that a later attempt can actually use?

Consider a small software product that never finds enough demand. Calling that a success would be dishonest. But suppose the person who built it had previously never taken a product from an idea to a public release. During the project, they had to decide what to leave out, put the product in front of users, and discover that the problem they expected people to care about was not urgent enough.

That does not rescue the product. It does change the next decision. The next time this person considers spending three months on an idea, they have a reason to test the problem earlier. They also have finished work that another person can inspect rather than a claim that they know how to build.

The same logic can apply outside a product. A small piece of paid work may never grow into an agency, a long-term contract, or a large source of income. Still, working for one real client can expose a difference between a task that sounds useful and a problem someone is actually willing to pay to remove. If that experience changes what kind of work the person offers next, or gives them credible evidence that they can deliver it, the project has altered the next starting position even if the project itself stays small.

This is easier to see when there is already a long track record behind the work. Someone with years of experience can begin a new project with skills, examples, relationships, and trust accumulated elsewhere. A person without much of that history has less to reuse. One finished piece of work can therefore matter for reasons that are modest but still concrete.

That does not mean everyone can simply choose the perfect project and accumulate their way out of every constraint. Money, health, access, timing, family responsibilities, customers, and luck can limit which opportunities are available in the first place. A project cannot guarantee that the next door opens. The narrower claim is that, when there is a choice between things to work on, it is reasonable to notice which choice is more likely to leave something usable if the direct outcome disappoints.

There is an obvious way to abuse this idea. Almost any bad project can be described afterward as a learning experience. A person can spend months avoiding users, never finish the work, repeat the same mistake, and still come away saying the time was valuable because they learned something about themselves.

That explanation deserves skepticism. The supposed benefit should be specific enough to affect a later action or create evidence that did not exist before. If the next decision is no different, the work is no more credible to anyone else, and nothing from the project can be used again, there may be very little reason to count the experience as progress.

This standard also prevents the argument from becoming an excuse to lower expectations. The best result is still for the work itself to succeed on its own terms. A product should solve a problem. Paid work should be worth paying for. A project that deserves to continue should be able to continue.

The second question matters because early work often has more than one consequence. It can produce an immediate result, and it can change what the person doing it is prepared to attempt afterward. Those consequences should not be confused, but neither should the second one be ignored.

Before committing serious time to a project, it can be useful to consider what is likely to remain if the main outcome falls short. A vague answer such as `experience` is weak. A concrete answer might be a finished piece of work, a better decision about the problem, or proof that changes what someone else is willing to trust you with.

That still does not make the project safe. It simply means the downside is easier to describe honestly.

Some projects end and leave the next attempt almost unchanged. Others end with modest results but still alter what can be done afterward. The difference is not the story told after the failure. It is whether the work actually changed the next starting point.
