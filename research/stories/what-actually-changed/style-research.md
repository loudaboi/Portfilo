## Assignment

Story ID: `C030`

Working label: `What actually changed?`

Content type: `Perspective`

Primary topic: `Building` — provisional until final package approval

Primary reader need: `Interpret`

## Story purpose

C030 should give a new reader a useful interpretation of why Portfilo's coverage areas belong together and what editorial question can connect them, without reproducing the About page, publishing a corporate mission statement, attacking other journalism, or turning the article into a founder biography.

The reader should leave understanding one bounded editorial idea:

An update can accurately establish that something happened while still leaving a second question whose answer may materially change what the development means in practice.

That idea is not a universal rule. Sometimes the update is the whole useful story.

Outside scope:

- a chronological founder story;
- claims that Philippine journalism or technology coverage is broadly broken;
- claims about what readers supposedly want without research;
- a list of Portfilo values or promises;
- a standards/methodology page in article form;
- a subscription/business-model pitch;
- a permanent tagline;
- using unresolved C001 or C021 findings as factual examples.

## Audience assumptions

Established by current Portfilo product copy:

- Portfilo is a business and technology publication;
- it covers Business, Technology, Startups, AI, and Building;
- it publishes multiple editorial types;
- it is for people curious about business and technology, including readers learning, working in, or building in those areas.

Source:

`src/pages/about.astro`

Editorial hypothesis for C030:

A reader may understand each topic individually while still benefiting from an explanation of why the publication places them together. This is a story-purpose decision, not a measured population claim.

No claim should be made that readers are confused, dissatisfied with existing journalism, or seeking a new publication unless separately researched.

## Subject / evidence shape

The material contains:

- an authored editorial judgment about how to follow business/technology developments;
- approved institutional facts from the current About page;
- actual Portfilo editorial behavior already demonstrated by published stories;
- generic/hypothetical development patterns that can be used only if clearly non-empirical;
- a meaningful limitation: short updates can be useful and no second layer is always required;
- no reported protagonist;
- no audience survey;
- no need for an external current-event hook;
- no factual basis for broad criticism of other publications.

This supports a concise `Perspective`: a bylined editorial argument with a concrete opening, an early thesis, a small number of examples, a real limiting principle, and a bounded ending.

It does **not** support a launch manifesto whose authority comes from repeated institutional promises.

## Professional form research

### Reference 1

Source/publication: Poynter

Title: `Seven Steps to a Strong Opinion`

URL: https://www.poynter.org/archive/2003/seven-steps-to-a-strong-opinion/

Access context: rechecked 2026-09-04.

#### Observed guidance

The piece argues against a stuffy, omnipotent institutional editorial voice and relays the instruction to `write essays, not editorials`. It also asks opinion writers to define the audience, tone, purpose and added value; contribute facts/context/reasoning rather than mere institutional authority; and attack their own premise before publishing.

#### Relevance to C030

This directly supports treating C030 as an **essay with an argument**, not a declaration of Portfilo's virtues. The publication's authority should come from a clear editorial distinction and concrete examples, not from `we believe`, `we will`, or `our mission` language.

The self-attack question is especially relevant because C030 needs to admit that an update is sometimes sufficient and that deeper treatment is not automatically better.

#### Limitation

This is general opinion/editorial guidance. It does not prescribe C030's exact opening, sentence rhythm, title, or institutional position.

### Reference 2

Source/publication: Poynter

Title: `Story Forms and Shapes`

URL: https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2008/story-forms-and-shapes/

Access context: rechecked 2026-09-04.

#### Observed guidance

The piece documents that journalism uses multiple forms rather than one universal story shape and treats short, reader-serving forms as legitimate alongside narrative and feature structures.

#### Relevance to C030

C030 does not need to become a formal manifesto, Q&A, listicle, or narrative just because it is a founding story. The material can use a short continuous essay structure because that form best exposes the editorial argument.

#### Limitation

The source is a broad form inventory, not a specific recipe for a founding editorial Perspective.

## Human-edited exemplars

The examples below were selected because they explain a publication's editorial lens or coverage logic to readers. They are **not** style authorities and are not all models to copy. Several are useful partly because they reveal launch-manifesto behaviors Portfilo should reject.

### Exemplar A — WIRED

Publication: WIRED

Title: `Welcome to the New WIRED`

Author: Gideon Lichfield

URL: https://www.wired.com/story/welcome-to-the-new-wired/

Date: 2022-01-03

Why this is comparable:

It is an editor-authored essay explaining how a technology publication understands its subject and why apparently different areas belong in the same editorial worldview.

Observed opening job:

It opens with a concrete disputed technology claim (blockchain) and uses the disagreement around it to establish the essay's central tension before turning to WIRED itself.

Answer/thesis position:

The core editorial argument arrives after the opening tension and historical context: technology cannot be understood through a simple good/bad binary, and the publication's job follows from that distinction.

Paragraph development:

Developed paragraphs carry one argumentative step at a time, often supported by examples of technologies, social effects, incentives, or the publication's history.

Evidence/example handling:

Examples are used to demonstrate the editorial problem before the essay states what the publication should do. Institutional claims are therefore partly earned by prior reasoning rather than appearing only as values declarations.

Transitions:

Mostly argumentative transitions from tension -> implication -> editorial consequence. The piece also uses explicit turns such as moving from critique to `what WIRED is for`.

First/second person use:

First person appears because the editor is explicitly taking responsibility for an editorial direction. Second person is present in the opening and reader-facing promises.

Rhetorical-question use:

High in the opening. Questions establish the binary tension but are not necessary to the underlying structure.

Subhead/section behavior:

Continuous essay; no conventional subheads in the main argument.

Counterargument/limitation handling:

The essay explicitly rejects both simple optimism and simple pessimism rather than attacking only one side.

Ending job:

Returns to an elevated formulation of where WIRED `lives` editorially.

Vocabulary/assumed knowledge:

Assumes familiarity with major technology debates and WIRED's history.

Patterns notably absent:

It is not organized as a five-topic coverage list or a standards checklist.

Transferable lesson:

**Demonstrate the editorial tension before declaring the publication's lens, and explain cross-topic coherence through mechanisms rather than category labels.**

What must not be imitated:

- blockchain binary setup;
- repeated either/or constructions;
- named slogan-like stance terms;
- rhetorical-question density;
- WIRED's history/house voice;
- elevated `where WIRED lives` closing cadence.

### Exemplar B — Rest of World

Publication: Rest of World

Title: `A letter from the founder`

Author: Sophie Schmidt

URL: https://restofworld.org/2020/letter-from-the-founder/

Date: 2020-04

Why this is comparable:

A founder explains the observation that connects a technology publication's coverage and eventually states the publication's mission.

Observed opening job:

Begins with direct personal experience across multiple places and a concrete observation about technology behaving differently in different contexts.

Answer/thesis position:

The editorial premise appears early, while the explicit publication mission is delayed until after several concrete examples.

Paragraph development:

The middle uses specific experiences and cases rather than a list of institutional values.

Evidence/example handling:

Concrete scenes and observations do most of the persuasive work before institutional self-description appears.

Transitions:

Move from personal observation -> examples -> information gap -> publication mission.

First/second person use:

Heavy first-person singular because the piece is explicitly autobiographical and founder-led; limited reader address appears at the end.

Rhetorical-question use:

Not a major structural device.

Subhead/section behavior:

Continuous short letter/essay.

Counterargument/limitation handling:

Limited; the piece is primarily a founding rationale rather than a self-attacking opinion essay.

Ending job:

Ends with mission, aspiration and an invitation to the reader.

Vocabulary/assumed knowledge:

General-reader technology vocabulary, with concrete geographic examples doing explanatory work.

Patterns notably absent:

It does not begin with a generic values list.

Transferable lesson:

**Institutional purpose reads more credibly after the essay has shown the underlying observation through concrete material.**

What must not be imitated:

- autobiographical travel narrative, because C030 has no comparable reported/personal evidence requirement;
- Rest of World's geographic lens;
- mission-statement phrasing;
- large population claims;
- invitation/call-to-action ending;
- distinctive metaphors and jokes.

### Exemplar C — 404 Media

Publication: 404 Media

Title: `Welcome to 404 Media`

Authors: Jason Koebler, Samantha Cole, Emanuel Maiberg, Joseph Cox

URL: https://www.404media.co/welcome-to-404-media/

Date: 2023-08-22

Why this is comparable:

A new independent publication explains what it will cover, how it thinks about reporting, and why its subjects/practices belong together.

Observed opening job:

Immediately introduces the publication and its ambitions.

Answer/thesis position:

Mission and business goals appear almost immediately rather than being earned through an essay argument.

Paragraph development:

The piece alternates between promises, concrete examples of launch stories, reporting philosophy, business model and subscription details.

Evidence/example handling:

One of its strongest moves is the early use of specific launch stories to demonstrate what the publication means by its stated ambitions.

Transitions:

Explicit institutional sections such as `Our point of view` and `Why are we doing this?`.

First/second person use:

Heavy `we` and direct reader address throughout.

Rhetorical-question use:

Limited, though subhead questions structure parts of the launch note.

Subhead/section behavior:

Strong institutional subheads divide editorial point of view from business rationale.

Counterargument/limitation handling:

The piece attacks a media-business model it rejects and proposes its own alternative; it is not strongly self-limiting.

Ending job:

Returns to the business proposition and reader relationship.

Vocabulary/assumed knowledge:

Informal, insider technology/media language.

Patterns notably absent:

The article does not hide what it is selling; it is openly a launch and subscription piece.

Transferable lesson:

**Concrete examples of actual published work are more persuasive than adjectives about what a publication intends to be.**

For C030, the already-published C009 can perform this job if one real Portfilo example is useful.

What must not be imitated:

- promise stacks (`we will...`);
- aggressive superiority claims;
- media-industry complaint as central rationale;
- subscription/business-model material;
- irreverent house voice;
- branded institutional subheads.

### Exemplar D — Semafor

Publication: Semafor

Title: `Welcome to Semafor`

Author: Ben Smith

URL: https://www.semafor.com/article/10/18/2022/welcome-to-semafor

Date: 2022-10-20

Why this is comparable:

A founder/editor explains an editorial model and the problem the publication says it is designed to address.

Observed opening job:

Starts with launch output and quickly names the publication's format and audience problem.

Answer/thesis position:

Institutional solution appears immediately.

Paragraph development:

Mixes personal editor history, audience claims, critique of the news environment, the product's editorial format, and external research.

Evidence/example handling:

Uses launch stories and Gallup data to support the case for the product.

Transitions:

The publication's own branded sections organize the piece.

First/second person use:

High first-person/institutional voice and direct address.

Rhetorical-question use:

Low; claims are primarily declarative.

Subhead/section behavior:

Uses Semafor's branded article structure as part of the argument itself.

Counterargument/limitation handling:

Includes a `Room for Disagreement` section acknowledging that other journalists may reject the approach.

Ending job:

Reader invitation and product expectation.

Vocabulary/assumed knowledge:

Media-industry language and explicit product terminology.

Patterns notably absent:

It does not pretend the format is neutral; the branded structure is openly part of the product.

Transferable lesson:

The willingness to acknowledge disagreement is useful. The **branded framework itself is not transferable** to C030.

What must not be imitated:

- unsupported audience claims without equivalent research;
- `news is broken` positioning;
- named/branded editorial framework;
- Semafor section names and cadence;
- product launch invitation.

## Cross-example findings

### Observed across references

1. Editorial-direction essays become more credible when a concrete tension, experience, example, or actual piece of work appears before or alongside the institutional claim.
2. The strongest comparable pieces explain **why the publication's subjects connect**, not merely what sections exist.
3. First-person institutional language is common because the publication is itself part of the subject, but the amount varies widely.
4. Several launch pieces end with an aspiration, promise, invitation or slogan. That behavior is common but not necessary to the writing job.
5. Several examples make claims about failures in existing media or unmet reader demand. Those claims require evidence and often dominate the launch rationale.
6. Named editorial frameworks and branded repeated questions can make a launch proposition easy to remember, but they also turn an editorial judgment into product marketing.

Material differences:

- WIRED develops an argument before stating institutional direction;
- Rest of World develops the mission from personal/reported experience;
- 404 Media declares the mission first and demonstrates it with launch stories;
- Semafor makes the editorial format itself part of the launch proposition.

There is no single universal founding-editorial form.

### Portfilo interpretation

Use:

- a concrete opening tension that belongs to the argument;
- thesis early, before the piece becomes a catalogue;
- a small number of examples that show how the editorial distinction changes what is worth asking;
- one actual Portfilo example only if it earns its space (C009 is the strongest available example);
- a real limiting principle: sometimes an update is sufficient;
- restrained institutional voice whose claims are traceable to About/product behavior or explicit Louda approval.

Reject:

- `why journalism needs Portfilo`;
- audience dissatisfaction claims;
- mission/promise lists;
- a five-topic tour where every coverage area gets symmetrical treatment;
- repeated use of `What actually changed?` as a branded framework;
- business-model or subscription explanation;
- autobiographical founder material invented because other founder letters use it;
- a grand final sentence about where Portfilo `lives`, the future of journalism, or changing the world.

## Proposed writing mechanics

Lead behavior:

Open with **one ordinary development pattern or one concrete Portfilo-relevant tension**, not a rapid list of four announcements. The opening must demonstrate the distinction between `the update` and `what the update changes` without implying the first is inadequate.

Thesis / answer timing:

State the central editorial argument within the first 3-5 paragraphs. Do not delay the Portfilo connection until the second half.

Paragraph development:

Use normal multi-sentence paragraphs. Each should advance one logical step: observation, implication, example, connection, limitation, or conclusion. Avoid dramatic one-line pacing as a default.

Example / evidence handling:

Use 2-3 developed examples maximum. Prefer examples that perform different jobs rather than a catalogue of topics. C009 may be used as an actual Portfilo example because its deployed article now demonstrates the move from an industry phrase to concrete roles. Generic AI/funding/product examples may be used as hypothetical patterns but must not become population claims.

Transition behavior:

Use logical transitions rooted in the subject. Avoid repeated formulas such as:

- `That is why...`;
- `The real question is...`;
- `What matters is...`;
- `In practice...`;
- `This is where...`;
- repeated `not X, but Y` turns.

First / second person limits:

First-person singular: none required. If used, at most a small number of moments where Louda explicitly owns an editorial judgment; do not create autobiographical material.

First-person plural / `Portfilo`: restrained and only for actual institutional claims supported by About/product behavior or the approved style brief.

Second person: avoid by default.

Rhetorical-question limits:

Do not use a string of questions as the opening. The title may remain a question candidate, but inside the body use at most one genuine rhetorical question if it performs necessary argumentative work. The article can make its point declaratively.

Subhead / section approach:

No subheads by default. At the target length, a continuous editorial essay better separates C030 from the About/Standards pages and from branded launch manifestos.

Counterargument / limitation placement:

Introduce the limiting principle after the main editorial distinction has been demonstrated but before the conclusion: short updates are useful; deeper treatment is warranted only when the additional question materially changes understanding.

Ending behavior:

End on the bounded editorial distinction or its practical implication. Do not add a subscription invitation, values promise, or elevated brand slogan. Do not repeat all five coverage areas or restate the thesis in grander language.

Terminology / vocabulary level:

Prefer concrete terms such as `workflow`, `cost`, `capacity`, `implementation`, `pricing`, `distribution`, `constraint`, `responsibility`, `operation`, and `decision` only where the example supports them.

Avoid corporate/editorial-strategy vocabulary such as:

- `editorial lens` in public prose unless unavoidable;
- `content ecosystem`;
- `intersection`;
- `operating reality` as a repeated catchphrase;
- `mission`;
- `value proposition`;
- `connect the dots`;
- `our approach`;
- `our philosophy`;
- `future-facing`;
- `practical intelligence`;
- `second-order effects` unless technically necessary.

## Anti-imitation boundary

Do not import:

- WIRED's binary metaphor, rhetorical-question cadence, named stance or `where WIRED lives` ending;
- Rest of World's personal travel scenes, global framing, mission wording, metaphor system or invitation;
- 404 Media's irreverent tone, promise stacks, business-model argument, explicit anti-industry rhetoric or subscription pitch;
- Semafor's `Semaform`/branded section logic, media-trust framing, reader-demand claims or named format terminology.

The exemplars authorize editorial **decisions**, not wording.
